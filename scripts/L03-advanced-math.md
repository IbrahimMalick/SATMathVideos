[[beat:title]] Welcome to the third lecture in our Digital S A T Math
series. Today, we will study Advanced Math. [[beat:recap]] In our previous
lectures, we built a foundation in linear algebra, problem solving, and data
analysis. We now move into nonlinear equations and nonlinear functions.
[[beat:third]] Advanced Math represents roughly one third of the Digital S A
T Math section. This makes it one of the most important areas of the exam.
[[beat:bridge]] It also creates a bridge between introductory algebra and
the higher level mathematics students may encounter in college, calculus,
engineering, science, technology, and other S T E M fields.

[[beat:corr]] Let us begin by examining the two main ways we can understand
a function. The first way is called the correspondence view. In the
correspondence view, a function acts like a machine. You provide an input.
The function applies a specific rule. It then produces exactly one output.
[[beat:feval]] For example, suppose a function is defined as follows. F of x
equals two x plus three. If the input is four, we replace x with four.
[[beat:feval2]] F of four equals two times four plus three. Two times four
is eight. Eight plus three is eleven. Therefore, the output is eleven.
[[beat:fnotation]] This view is especially important when working with
function notation. You must be comfortable evaluating a function when the
input is a number. You must also be able to evaluate a function when the
input is another expression. [[beat:fxa]] For example, suppose you are asked
to find F of x plus a. You must replace every x in the function with the
complete expression x plus a. If F of x equals two x plus three, then F of x
plus a equals two times the quantity x plus a, plus three. [[beat:fxa2]]
After distributing the two, the result is two x plus two a plus three.
[[beat:mistake]] A common mistake is to replace only part of the original
expression. Remember that the complete input must be substituted into every
place where x appears.

[[beat:cov]] The second way to understand a function is called the
covariation view. In the covariation view, we study how two quantities
change together. We ask what happens to one variable as the other variable
changes. [[beat:linear_rate]] A linear function changes at a constant rate.
For example, if the output increases by three every time the input increases
by one, the rate of change is constant. [[beat:quad_rate]] A nonlinear
function behaves differently. Its rate of change does not remain constant. A
quadratic function has a changing rate of change. However, the change in
that rate follows a consistent pattern. In a table of values for a quadratic
function, the first differences may change, but the second differences
remain constant. [[beat:expo]] An exponential function follows another
pattern. Instead of increasing by a fixed amount, the output is multiplied
by a fixed factor. For example, consider the sequence two, six, eighteen,
and fifty four. Each value is three times the previous value. This is an
exponential pattern because the values are multiplied by a constant factor
of three. [[beat:families]] Understanding these patterns can help you
identify the family of a function from a table, graph, or word problem.

[[beat:real]] Now let us discuss how nonlinear functions model the real
world. Linear functions are useful when a quantity changes by a constant
amount. However, many real world relationships do not follow straight
lines. [[beat:ball]] Quadratic functions can model the path of a ball thrown
into the air. The ball rises, reaches a maximum height, and then falls.
[[beat:profit]] A business may also use a quadratic function to model
profit. Profit may increase up to a certain point and then decrease if
production costs become too high. [[beat:expo_real]] Exponential functions
describe growth and decay. They can be used to model compound interest,
population growth, the spread of disease, radioactive decay, and the
decreasing value of equipment. [[beat:poly]] Polynomial and rational
functions can describe more complex relationships. Engineers may use these
functions to model curved structures, changing speeds, density, volume, and
other physical systems. [[beat:interpret]] When you see a word problem, do
not immediately search for a formula. First, interpret the situation. Ask
yourself how the quantity is changing. [[beat:ask_list]] Is it increasing by
the same amount each time? If so, the relationship may be linear. Is it
increasing or decreasing by the same percentage or factor? If so, the
relationship may be exponential. Does the graph rise and then fall? Or does
it fall and then rise? If so, the relationship may be quadratic.
[[beat:pattern_note]] Identifying the pattern is often more important than
memorising a formula.

[[beat:cart]] Now let us discuss the connection between an equation and its
graph. A point is written using an x coordinate and a y coordinate. A point
lies on a graph if its coordinates make the equation true. This relationship
is sometimes called the Cartesian connection. [[beat:cart_ex]] Suppose the
equation is y equals x squared plus one. We want to determine whether the
point two comma five is on the graph. [[beat:cart_on]] Replace x with two.
Two squared plus one equals four plus one. The result is five. The y
coordinate is also five. Therefore, the point two comma five lies on the
graph. [[beat:cart_off]] Now consider the point two comma six. When x equals
two, the equation produces a y value of five, not six. Therefore, the point
two comma six does not lie on the graph. [[beat:cart_note]] This connection
between equations and graphs is extremely important on the Digital S A T. It
allows you to test points, interpret intercepts, and connect algebraic
expressions with visual information.

[[beat:forms]] Advanced Math also requires you to recognise the structure of
an equation. A quadratic function can be written in several forms. Each form
reveals different information about the graph. [[beat:standard]] The first
form is standard form. Standard form is written as: A x squared plus b x
plus c. The value of c gives the y intercept. This is because the y
intercept occurs when x equals zero. When x equals zero, the terms
containing x disappear, leaving only c. [[beat:standard_ex]] For example,
consider the function: Y equals two x squared minus five x plus seven. The y
intercept is seven. [[beat:factored]] The second form is factored form.
Factored form may be written as: A times the quantity x minus r, times the
quantity x minus s. This form reveals the roots, also called the zeros or x
intercepts. The roots occur when the output equals zero. A product equals
zero when at least one factor equals zero. [[beat:factored_ex]] For example,
consider the function: Y equals the quantity x minus three, times the
quantity x plus two. The first factor equals zero when x equals three. The
second factor equals zero when x equals negative two. Therefore, the x
intercepts are three and negative two. [[beat:vertexform]] The third form is
vertex form. Vertex form is written as: A times the quantity x minus h
squared, plus k. This form reveals the vertex. The vertex is the point h
comma k. The vertex is the maximum or minimum point of the parabola. If the
value of a is positive, the parabola opens upward and the vertex is a
minimum. If the value of a is negative, the parabola opens downward and the
vertex is a maximum. [[beat:vertex_ex]] For example, consider the function:
Y equals two times the quantity x minus four squared, plus one. The vertex
is four comma one. Because the coefficient two is positive, the parabola
opens upward. Therefore, the vertex is the minimum point. [[beat:which]]
Success on the Digital S A T often depends on recognising which form of a
quadratic is most useful. Use standard form when you need the y intercept.
Use factored form when you need the roots or x intercepts. Use vertex form
when you need the maximum, minimum, or vertex. [[beat:rewrite]] You may also
need to rewrite a quadratic from one form into another. This is not simply
an algebra exercise. The purpose is to reveal the information hidden inside
the equation.

[[beat:parent]] Now let us discuss function families and transformations. A
parent function is the simplest version of a function. For example, the
parent quadratic function is: Y equals x squared. More complicated quadratic
functions are transformations of this basic graph. A transformation moves,
stretches, compresses, or reflects a graph. [[beat:vshift]] Suppose we add a
constant to the entire function. For example: Y equals x squared plus three.
The graph of y equals x squared moves upward by three units. If we subtract
three from the entire function, the graph moves downward by three units.
This is called a vertical shift. [[beat:hshift]] Now consider a change
inside the input. For example: Y equals the quantity x minus four squared.
This graph moves four units to the right. The minus sign can feel confusing
because the graph moves in the positive direction. To understand this, ask
what input makes the squared expression equal zero. The quantity x minus
four equals zero when x equals four. Therefore, the vertex moves from zero
comma zero to four comma zero. [[beat:hshift2]] Now consider: Y equals the
quantity x plus four squared. The expression x plus four equals zero when x
equals negative four. Therefore, the graph moves four units to the left.
[[beat:shift_rule]] Remember this rule. Changes outside the function move
the graph vertically. Changes inside the input move the graph horizontally.
Inside horizontal shifts operate in the opposite direction of the sign you
see. You can always confirm the direction by finding the input that makes
the inside expression equal zero. [[beat:stretch]] Functions can also be
stretched, compressed, or reflected. Consider: Y equals three x squared. The
graph becomes narrower because all output values are multiplied by three.
[[beat:compress]] Now consider: Y equals one half x squared. The graph
becomes wider because the output values are multiplied by one half.
[[beat:reflect]] Consider: Y equals negative x squared. The negative sign
reflects the graph across the x axis. Instead of opening upward, the
parabola opens downward.

[[beat:reverse]] Now let us discuss the strategic power of reverse thinking.
Students often approach functions in one direction. They are given an x
value and calculate the corresponding y value. However, many advanced
questions require the reverse process. You may be given an output and asked
to find the input that produced it. [[beat:rev_eq]] Suppose: F of x equals
three x plus two. We are told that F of x equals seventeen. Instead of
calculating an output, we must find the input. Set three x plus two equal to
seventeen. [[beat:rev_solve]] Subtract two from both sides. Three x equals
fifteen. [[beat:rev_answer]] Divide by three. X equals five.
[[beat:rev_note]] This type of reverse reasoning is closely connected to
solving equations and understanding inverse relationships. It prevents you
from simply moving symbols without understanding their meaning.

[[beat:questions]] When solving Advanced Math questions, ask yourself: What
information is given? What information is hidden? Which form of the function
reveals what I need? What input produces this output? What does this
coefficient represent? What does this point mean on the graph? Is the
pattern linear, quadratic, exponential, polynomial, or rational? These
questions help you solve problems efficiently and accurately.

[[beat:review]] Let us review the main ideas from this lecture. Advanced
Math focuses on nonlinear equations and functions. [[beat:rev1]] A function
can be viewed as a machine that connects each input to exactly one output.
It can also be viewed as a relationship between quantities that change
together. [[beat:rev2]] Linear functions change by a constant amount.
Quadratic functions have constant second differences. Exponential functions
change by a constant factor or percentage. [[beat:rev3]] A point lies on a
graph when its coordinates make the equation true. [[beat:rev4]] Standard
form reveals the y intercept. Factored form reveals the roots or x
intercepts. Vertex form reveals the maximum or minimum point. [[beat:rev5]]
Changes outside a function create vertical shifts. Changes inside the input
create horizontal shifts. [[beat:rev6]] Reverse thinking helps you find the
input needed to produce a particular output. [[beat:key]] The key idea from
this lecture is simple. Do not treat Advanced Math as a collection of
unrelated formulas. Focus on the relationships between equations, graphs,
tables, and real world situations. When you understand the structure of a
function, you can choose the most efficient path to the answer.
