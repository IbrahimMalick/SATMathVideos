[[beat:title]] Welcome back. Building on our foundation in algebra, we now
move to the second major math domain of the digital S A T. Problem Solving
and Data Analysis. [[beat:recap]] In our first math lecture, we focused on
equations, variables, and linear relationships. In this lecture, we will
study how mathematics is used to understand real world situations.
[[beat:topics]] This domain focuses on data, ratios, rates, percentages,
probability, and statistical reasoning. It is less about manipulating
complicated symbols and more about using numbers to describe and analyse the
world around us. These skills are valuable in science, technology, business,
healthcare, education, and many other careers.

[[beat:prop]] Let us begin with proportional relationships. Many students
think that a proportion is simply a problem where two fractions are set
equal and then cross multiplied. Cross multiplication can be useful, but
strong S A T students understand the relationship behind the numbers.
[[beat:prop_def]] A proportional relationship exists when two quantities
change at a constant rate. [[beat:event]] For example, imagine that the
total cost of an event depends on the number of participants. If the event
costs twenty pounds per person, the total cost increases by twenty pounds
each time another person joins. [[beat:constant]] The number twenty is
called the constant of proportionality. It connects the number of
participants to the total cost. [[beat:event_values]] If ten people attend,
the cost is two hundred pounds. If fifty people attend, the cost is one
thousand pounds. The numbers change, but the relationship remains constant.
[[beat:rates]] On the S A T, proportional reasoning often appears in ratio,
rate, and unit conversion questions. You may be asked to convert miles per
hour into feet per second. You may need to calculate the density of an
object. You may also need to compare prices, distances, times, or
quantities. [[beat:units]] The key is to treat units as part of the
calculation. Units are not simply labels. They can be multiplied, divided,
and cancelled in the same way as numbers. This process is called unit
analysis. [[beat:speed]] For example, suppose a car travels sixty miles in
two hours. To find the average speed, divide sixty miles by two hours.
[[beat:speed_result]] The result is thirty miles per hour. The unit miles
per hour tells us exactly what the number represents. Always include the
units while solving the problem. They can help you decide whether you should
multiply or divide.

[[beat:pct]] Now let us discuss percentages. Percentages are among the most
common mathematical ideas in everyday life. We use them to calculate sales
tax, tips, discounts, interest, growth, and loss of value. The S A T tests
both basic and advanced percentage problems. [[beat:pct_def]] A percentage
means a quantity out of one hundred. For example, twenty percent means
twenty out of one hundred. It can also be written as the decimal zero point
two. [[beat:pct_change]] However, the S A T often tests percentage change
rather than a simple percentage of a number. Suppose a piece of equipment
loses thirty six percent of its value every year. A common mistake is to
subtract thirty six from the value. That is not correct. Thirty six percent
is not a fixed amount. It is a portion of the current value.
[[beat:keep64]] If the equipment loses thirty six percent, it keeps sixty
four percent of its value. Sixty four percent written as a decimal is zero
point six four. Therefore, each year, the new value is zero point six four
times the previous value. [[beat:decay1]] If the equipment is worth one
thousand pounds today, its value after one year is six hundred and forty
pounds. [[beat:decay2]] During the following year, the thirty six percent
decrease is calculated from six hundred and forty pounds, not from the
original one thousand pounds. This creates a multiplicative relationship.
Understanding this idea will help you later when you study exponential
growth and exponential decay. [[beat:pct_rule]] Remember this rule. A
percentage increase or decrease is based on the original amount for that
particular change. It is not usually a fixed number.

[[beat:multi]] Now let us move to multivariable thinking. In previous
algebra lessons, we studied how one variable changes in relation to another
variable. For example, we may study how the variable y changes when the
variable x changes. In Problem Solving and Data Analysis, you may need to
consider several factors at the same time. [[beat:sleep]] Imagine a study
that examines how much sleep students receive. The study may not look only
at the number of hours each student sleeps. It may also compare students by
age, year group, study habits, or other characteristics. This means that you
must examine the data in context. You may need to determine whether one
group sleeps more than another group. You may need to decide whether the
groups have similar averages but different levels of variation.

[[beat:charts]] This is why data representation is important. On the digital
S A T, data may be presented in tables, scatterplots, dot plots, bar graphs,
or box plots. [[beat:centre]] You may be asked to compare the centre of two
datasets. The centre is commonly measured using the mean or the median. The
mean is calculated by adding all the values and dividing by the number of
values. The median is the middle value when the numbers are placed in order.
[[beat:spread]] You may also be asked to compare the spread of the data.
Spread tells us how far the values are distributed from one another. Common
measures of spread include the range and standard deviation. The range is
the difference between the highest value and the lowest value.
[[beat:stdev]] Standard deviation measures how far the values tend to be
from the mean. [[beat:stdev_small]] A smaller standard deviation means that
the values are closer together. [[beat:stdev_large]] A larger standard
deviation means that the values are more spread out. [[beat:stdev_note]] You
do not normally need to calculate standard deviation by hand on the S A T.
However, you must understand what it tells you about a dataset.

[[beat:prob]] Now let us discuss probability and statistical inference.
Probability is the study of uncertainty and random events. [[beat:coin]]
Suppose you flip a fair coin. You cannot know with certainty whether the
next result will be heads or tails. However, if you flip the coin one
thousand times, you would expect the results to be roughly half heads and
half tails. Probability does not always predict an individual result.
Instead, it describes long term patterns. [[beat:dice]] A basic probability
is calculated by dividing the number of favourable outcomes by the total
number of possible outcomes. For example, a standard six sided number cube
has six possible outcomes. Only one side shows the number four.
[[beat:dice_result]] Therefore, the probability of rolling a four is one out
of six.

[[beat:inference]] Statistical inference works in the opposite direction.
Inference uses information from a sample to make a conclusion about a larger
population. A population is the entire group we want to study. A sample is
the smaller group from which we collect information. [[beat:survey]] For
example, a company may have four hundred employees. It may survey twenty
employees to learn how the entire workforce feels about a new policy. The
twenty employees are the sample. The four hundred employees are the
population. [[beat:random]] However, the conclusion is only reliable if the
sample represents the population. This is where random selection becomes
important. A random sample gives every member of the population a fair
chance of being selected. [[beat:biased]] Suppose the company surveys only
employees from one department. The results may not represent the entire
company. That department may have different experiences from the other
departments. However, if the twenty employees are randomly selected from
across the company, the sample is more likely to represent the full
workforce. [[beat:questions]] When evaluating a study, ask these questions.
How was the sample selected? Is the sample large enough? Does the sample
represent the population? Was there any bias in the selection process?
[[beat:generalise]] The S A T may ask which population a study can
reasonably describe. The answer depends on how the sample was chosen. A
random sample allows researchers to generalise results to the larger
population from which the sample was selected. [[beat:causation]] However,
random selection does not automatically prove that one factor caused another
factor. To establish cause and effect, researchers generally need a properly
designed experiment with random assignment. [[beat:distinction]] This is an
important distinction. Random selection helps researchers generalise from a
sample to a population. Random assignment helps researchers determine
whether a treatment caused a result.

[[beat:investigator]] Now let us bring these ideas together. Success in
Problem Solving and Data Analysis requires active thinking. When you see a
graph, table, or word problem, do not immediately search for an answer
choice that looks correct. First, ask what the data is showing.
[[beat:ask_list]] What quantities are being compared? What are the units? Is
the relationship proportional? Is the change additive or multiplicative? Is
the relationship linear or exponential? What does the mean or median tell
us? How spread out are the values? Was the sample selected randomly? Does
the evidence support the conclusion? [[beat:investigator2]] Approach each
problem like an investigator. Your job is not simply to calculate. Your job
is to understand the situation and decide which mathematical tool is
appropriate.

[[beat:review]] Let us review the main ideas from this lecture.
[[beat:rev1]] A proportional relationship has a constant rate of change
between two quantities. [[beat:rev2]] Units should be treated as part of the
calculation. [[beat:rev3]] Percentage change is multiplicative and is based
on the relevant starting value. [[beat:rev4]] Data analysis involves
comparing the centre and spread of datasets. [[beat:rev5]] Probability
describes patterns in random events. [[beat:rev6]] Statistical inference
uses a sample to make conclusions about a population. [[beat:rev7]] Random
selection supports generalisation. Random assignment supports conclusions
about cause and effect. [[beat:key]] The key idea from this lecture is
simple. Do not look only at the numbers. Understand the relationship the
numbers represent. [[beat:next]] In our next lesson, we will move into
Advanced Math. We will apply these reasoning skills to nonlinear equations
and functions. [[beat:end]] That completes this introduction to Problem
Solving and Data Analysis.
