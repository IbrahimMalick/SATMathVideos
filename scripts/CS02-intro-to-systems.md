[[beat:open]] Okay, so today we begin computer science. But I'm not going to
begin by talking about computers. I'm going to begin with something much
bigger. [[beat:everything_system]] Look around you. Your school is a system.
Your family is a system. Your car or your motorcycle is a system. The human
body — our heart, our brain, our kidneys — this is a whole system. Your
mobile phone is a system. The internet that we are using now, that is a
system as well. And of course a computer is a system.
[[beat:system_def]] So before we understand a computer, we have to
understand one very important word, and that word is system. A system is a
collection of interconnected components that work together to achieve a
common objective. [[beat:parts_purpose]] So there are two words I want you
to lock into your memory: parts plus purpose. No parts, no system. No
purpose, no meaningful system. [[beat:car_parts]] Imagine I take the engine
of a car, four tyres, a steering wheel and a battery, and throw all of them
into a room. Do I have a car? No, I don't have a car. I have all the parts
of the car, but I don't have a car, do I? I have components.
[[beat:car_objective]] Now for those components to become a system, they
must be connected, they must interact, and they must work toward an
objective. What will be its objective? The objective of the car is
transportation. [[beat:computer_parts]] Now imagine your computer. Keyboard,
mouse, CPU, memory, storage, monitor, operating system, applications. None
of these alone is the computer system. You will say, sir, this is not a
computer, it is a keyboard. When I put these things together and put an
operating system on them, then it becomes one system.
[[beat:together]] They work together. You press a key, the computer receives
input, the processor works on it, memory holds the information, and the
monitor shows the result. Again, parts working together for a purpose. If
you remember just that idea, much of this chapter becomes easy.

[[beat:systems_theory]] Now let's talk about systems theory. Systems theory
is essentially a way of studying complicated things by looking at them as
systems: their parts, their relationships, how they interact, and how they
change over time. Instead of studying only one individual component, systems
theory asks: how does everything work together? [[beat:body_example]] Think
about the human body. A doctor cannot completely understand the body simply
by studying the heart. Yes, there are cardiologists who specialize in the
heart. But the MBBS doctor is understanding your whole body. The heart
interacts with blood vessels. The lungs provide oxygen. The nervous system
sends signals. The digestive system provides nutrients. The brain
coordinates activities. The body works because its subsystems interact.
[[beat:ocec]] Now, according to our chapter, we can describe a system using
four fundamental ideas: objective, components, environment and
communication. If you want, you can remember it as O-C-E-C.

[[beat:objective]] Now we will see them separately. What is objective? Every
system has an objective, a purpose, a goal. As we discussed earlier,
transportation was the goal of the vehicle. If you have installed a
thermostat, its goal is to keep your room at the desired temperature. A
computer processes data and performs tasks. A school provides education.
Whenever you are asked about a system, your first question should be: what
is this system trying to accomplish? [[beat:objective_cats]] The textbook
gives us three useful categories of objectives. One is information
processing. A computer collects, stores, processes and produces information.
Your brain also processes information received through the senses. The
second is supporting another system. Your smartphone provides a platform on
which apps operate. The third is achieving a specific goal. A thermostat
maintains a temperature. A car engine converts energy so that the vehicle
can move. [[beat:no_destination]] Remember this: a system without an
objective is like a journey without a destination.
[[beat:components]] The second element is components. Components are the
individual building blocks of the system. Think about a football team.
Goalkeeper, defenders, midfielders, forwards. They have different roles, but
they contribute to one objective. A computer is exactly the same idea.
Keyboard has one role, CPU another, RAM another, storage another, monitor
another. Different jobs, but one system.
[[beat:environment]] The third thing this chapter tells us is environment.
Now we move outside the system. A system does not normally exist in complete
isolation. It operates inside an environment. The environment means the
external factors that interact with or influence the system.
[[beat:env_examples]] For a computer, the environment can include its user,
electricity, network connection and external devices. Right now we are on
Zoom, but if the electricity goes off, and if your internet goes off, then
we can't talk. We have the system, but our external environment is not
working for us. In the same way, the environment of a tree is sunlight,
water, soil, temperature and other organisms. This gives us an important
idea: the system is inside, the environment is outside, but the two
interact. [[beat:communication]] The fourth element is communication.
Suppose your keyboard detects that you pressed the letter A, but it has no
way of communicating this information to the computer. What happens?
Nothing. Communication is what allows components to coordinate their
actions. In a human body, nerves carry signals. In computers, buses and
circuits carry information. In networks, cables, wireless signals and
protocols enable communication.

[[beat:static_dynamic]] Now we have to talk about how systems get more
sophisticated. This chapter talks about the environment and contrasts them.
Static versus dynamic. Static means the environment does not normally change
by itself while the system is operating. Dynamic means the environment can
change independently. [[beat:weather]] Think of the weather. Temperature
changes, humidity changes, wind changes, rain begins and stops. That's a
highly dynamic environment, so a weather monitoring system has to
continuously respond to those changes. [[beat:deterministic]] Then
deterministic versus non-deterministic. Deterministic means predictable. If
the same conditions produce a known result, we can predict what will happen.
Non-deterministic means there is uncertainty or randomness.
[[beat:d_definite]] So here is your memory trick. Deterministic means D,
definite. Both begin with D. Non-deterministic means the exact outcome
cannot always be predicted. [[beat:dont_confuse]] Do not confuse those two
pairs. Static and dynamic ask: does the environment change? Deterministic
and non-deterministic ask: can we predict the result? That distinction can
save you a lot of marks.

[[beat:natural_artificial]] Now let's move on to natural and artificial
systems. Systems can be broadly divided into natural systems and artificial
systems. This is extremely easy if you remember one question: who made it?
If it exists naturally without being created by humans, it is a natural
system. If humans deliberately created it, it is an artificial system.
[[beat:natural_kinds]] Natural systems exist in nature and function
according to natural processes. First, physical systems: atoms, stars,
planets, galaxies and other physical structures. Second, chemical systems.
Think about substances interacting and forming new substances. Water is a
familiar example: hydrogen and oxygen participate in a chemical process to
become H2O. Third, biological systems: living organisms, cells, organs,
plants, animals, the human body — growth, reproduction and metabolism.
Fourth, psychological systems. These relate to the mind, behaviour,
thoughts and emotions. [[beat:levels]] So our natural system chain is
physical, chemical, biological and psychological. You do not need to turn
this into something frightening. Think of levels: matter, chemistry, life,
mind. That gives you the sequence.
[[beat:artificial]] Now, artificial systems. Artificial systems are
deliberately designed and constructed by human beings to perform particular
functions or solve problems. [[beat:artificial_kinds]] In this chapter I
will tell you about three major groups which are worth remembering:
knowledge systems, engineering systems and social systems.
[[beat:knowledge]] First of all, let's talk about knowledge systems.
Knowledge systems deal with information or knowledge. Mathematics is a
knowledge system. Logic is a knowledge system. Databases organize
information, so that's a knowledge system. Information management systems
capture, organize, archive and distribute information. Think of a library
database. There may be thousands of books. Without organization, information
becomes difficult to use. [[beat:engineering]] Engineering systems are
designed solutions. A bridge is an engineering system. A water treatment
plant is an engineering system. A robotic arm in a factory is an engineering
system. A home automation system is an engineering system. Software itself
can also form an engineering system. Engineering takes knowledge and uses it
to build solutions. [[beat:social]] How about social systems? A social
system organizes human activity and relationships. A school is a social
system, a university is a social system, government is a social system, a
corporation is a social system. Each has people, each has roles, each has
rules, each has objectives. [[beat:systems_thinking]] Notice what computer
science is teaching us here. A system does not have to contain wires and
processors. A university can be a system. A country can contain systems. The
internet is a system. Your body is a system. This is why systems thinking is
powerful.

[[beat:science]] After that, this chapter tells us about systems and
science. The chapter asks us to connect systems and science. There are two
approaches we need to distinguish: natural science and design science.
[[beat:understand_create]] Here's the simplest possible distinction. Natural
science studies what exists. Design science creates what should exist.
Natural science studies what is there in this world, in this universe, in
our body. Design science thinks about what we should make. Do we need
wheels? Do we need a bicycle? Do we need a car? Do we need a microwave? To
create these things, we have to do design science. So let me repeat that.
Natural science tries to understand. Design science tries to create.
[[beat:forest]] Suppose scientists enter a forest. If they study the
ecosystem to understand how species interact, that is natural science. But
imagine they discover a problem and then develop a new software system to
monitor the forest and protect endangered species. Now they are doing design
science. Natural science is mainly descriptive. Design science is mainly
prescriptive. [[beat:cycles]] The textbook illustrates natural science
through an empirical cycle: observation, question, hypothesis, experiment,
analysis, conclusion. Design science uses a different cycle. A problem is
identified, a solution is designed, the solution is implemented, and then
the solution is evaluated. And if it does not work well enough, improve it.
[[beat:cs_fits]] Now you must be thinking, where does computer science fit
in here? Computer science uses both. Sometimes computer scientists study
existing algorithms. For example, which sorting algorithm is faster? Which
uses less memory? Why does one perform better with certain types of data?
That is closer to the natural science side: studying and understanding. But
other computer scientists want to create something — a programming language,
an application, an algorithm, a database, an artificial intelligence system.
That is design science. [[beat:not_just_using]] So computer science does not
simply mean using computers. It includes understanding computation and
designing computational solutions. If it was only using a computer, then
your grandparents who are using WhatsApp on mobile phones would also be
computer scientists, but they are not, they are consumers.

[[beat:computer_system]] Okay, so let's move to the computer as a system.
Everything we have learned comes together now. Our chapter says a computer
is a complex system designed to process data and perform tasks according to
instructions. Now notice how perfectly it fits our system definition. It has
an objective, it has components, its components communicate, and it operates
within an environment. [[beat:computer_objective]] The computer's objective
is to process data, perform calculations and execute tasks efficiently. When
you open a browser, write a document, watch a video or play a game, the
computer is executing instructions.
[[beat:interface_components]] The chapter groups computer components in
useful ways. First are interface components. Input devices allow information
to enter: keyboard, mouse, microphone. Output devices communicate results:
monitor, printer. [[beat:cpu]] Then we have the processing components. The
most important is the CPU, the central processing unit. Think of the CPU as
the component responsible for carrying out instructions.
[[beat:ram]] Then we have RAM, random access memory. RAM temporarily holds
the information and instructions currently being used. RAM is the computer's
working area, not its permanent warehouse. It's a working area, not a
warehouse. When you're actively working on something, RAM helps keep the
necessary data quickly accessible. [[beat:storage_os_apps]] Permanent
storage, like an SSD or hard drive, retains data for later use. Then comes
the operating system. It coordinates the computer's hardware and provides
the environment in which applications operate. And applications perform
specific user tasks — for example a word processor, a browser, a game, a
calculator.

[[beat:buses]] Now let's look at the communication components. The
motherboard interconnects the computer's major components. The computer also
uses buses to carry information. If you want to understand the bus, then
understand it as that which connects. There are three names you should
remember: data bus, address bus and control bus. If you want a memory trick,
you can use D-A-C. [[beat:dac_meaning]] Now what does the data bus do? It
transports data. The address bus identifies where information should go or
come from. And the control bus carries control signals.
[[beat:delivery]] Think of it like this. The package that you are getting,
that is the data. The address of the home where it is being delivered is the
destination. And the instructions on how to deliver it are the control
signals. So: data, address and control. [[beat:interaction]] Imagine you
double-click a document. Your mouse receives your action. That input reaches
the computer. The operating system recognizes your request. The required
application is launched. Instructions and data are moved into memory. The
CPU processes them. Information is ultimately sent to the display. One
simple action by the user may involve multiple components working together.
That is precisely why it is called a computer system.
[[beat:environment2]] Okay, remember what environment means? Everything
external that affects or interacts with the system. For a computer, examples
include power supply — without electricity, you can't operate it. Network,
whether it is a wired cable or wireless. Peripheral devices like printers,
scanners, external storage. The user is very important too. You type
something, the computer responds. You request a web page, the computer
communicates over a network. So the computer does not simply sit there
operating independently. It continuously interacts with the environment.

[[beat:von_neumann]] I want you to imagine that we are opening the computer
and looking at the basic model of how instructions actually get executed.
That model is the von Neumann architecture. This architecture is associated
with mathematician John von Neumann and the development of stored program
computers. [[beat:vn_components]] Now the textbook highlights four main
components: memory, CPU, input devices and output devices. Let's understand
them. Memory contains data and program instructions needed for processing.
[[beat:alu_cu]] Inside the CPU, two important components are emphasized. One
is the ALU, the arithmetic logic unit. It performs arithmetic and logical
operations: addition, subtraction, comparisons, logical decisions. Then we
have the CU, the control unit. The control unit coordinates and controls
execution. Think about an orchestra. The musicians actually produce the
sounds, but the conductor coordinates when things happen. The control unit
performs a similar coordination role. [[beat:io_flow]] Input devices allow
data and instructions to enter. Output devices communicate the processed
results. So imagine: input, process, output, with memory supporting the
process. [[beat:fdes]] Now comes the sequence that students absolutely must
know. Fetch, decode, execute, store. Say it as one rhythm. Fetch. Decode.
Execute. Store. You should be able to hear that sequence in your head during
the examination. [[beat:fetch]] Fetch: the CPU retrieves an instruction from
memory. The textbook introduces two important registers here, the PC,
program counter, and the IR, instruction register. The program counter keeps
the address of the next instruction. The instruction is fetched and placed
into the instruction register. Simple memory trick: PC points, IR holds. The
PC points toward the next instruction. The IR holds the current instruction.
[[beat:decode]] Now the control unit examines the instruction and determines
what needs to happen. That is decoding. Think of somebody giving you an
instruction in English. Before performing it, you must understand what it
means. If you speak Urdu or Punjabi, whatever your native tongue, you will
first understand the English in your own tongue. The CPU does the same.
[[beat:execute]] Then let's go to execute. What happens in execution? The
instruction is actually carried out. If arithmetic is required, the ALU will
perform the operation. If data must be moved, the relevant components
coordinate that movement. [[beat:store]] Then store. Finally, the result can
be stored in memory or sent to an output device. Fetch, decode, execute,
store. If you remember this sequence by the end of the day, you will have
done a very good job.

[[beat:vn_characteristics]] Let's talk from the point of view of the exam.
Let me tell you about the characteristics of von Neumann architecture. This
chapter tells three things. First, a single memory store: program
instructions and data share memory. Second, sequential execution:
instructions are normally processed one after another. Step one, then step
two, then step three. [[beat:stored_program]] Third is the stored program
concept. Programs themselves are stored in memory. This allows programs and
instructions to be changed rather than building a completely new machine
every time. [[beat:advantages]] Now if you ask me about its advantages, one
of its advantages is its simplified design: data and instructions can share
memory. Its second advantage is flexibility. Programs can be changed. You
can install new software, update software, run completely different
applications on the same computer. [[beat:flexibility_example]] Imagine if
buying Microsoft Word required a completely different computer. Then Chrome
required another computer, and a game required another computer. The stored
program concept gives us flexibility.
[[beat:bottleneck]] After that, this chapter tells us about disadvantages.
What is the von Neumann bottleneck? This is also important, because in most
questions advantages and disadvantages are asked together. Data and
instructions depend on a shared pathway and memory architecture. The CPU may
be capable of operating very quickly, but access to instructions and data
can limit overall performance. [[beat:motorway]] Imagine a huge motorway
merging into a single narrow toll gate. It does not matter how fast the cars
can travel. The narrow point creates a bottleneck. That is the idea.
[[beat:security]] Another disadvantage mentioned in the chapter is security
risk, because instructions and data reside within the same general memory
architecture, and malicious modification of instructions can create
problems.

[[beat:computing_systems]] A computing system consists of hardware and
software working together to process data and perform tasks. You can see my
face, you can hear my voice, when you are chatting with me, all these things
are working. What are we using? Look at the hardware — either you are on
your phone or on your computer. Software: you have Zoom installed, I have
Zoom installed. We also have electricity, and internet is also available.
[[beat:hardware_software]] So what does hardware mean? Even if you are using
a phone, it also has a CPU. The computer also has a CPU. It also has RAM and
storage. If you are using a computer, it has a physical keyboard. If you are
using a phone, it has a software-based keyboard. You have a monitor or a
screen. Now what is the meaning of software? The instructions. There is the
operating system, either on a Windows machine or macOS or Android or iOS.
Then there are applications on your devices. You have an application for
Zoom. WhatsApp will also be running, Facebook, Instagram, TikTok. Then you
also have power, either electricity or battery power.
[[beat:kinds]] After that, this chapter identifies several kinds of
computing systems, including computers, software systems, computer networks
and the internet. [[beat:networks]] What is a computer network? A computer
network connects computers and other devices. Why? There are three major
reasons: for resource sharing, for communication, and for data management
and collaboration. A network includes hardware such as routers, switches and
cables, and software such as protocols and network operating systems.
[[beat:lan_wan]] You should already know two familiar classifications: LAN,
local area network, and WAN, wide area network. A LAN normally connects
devices across a limited geographical area, for example a school computer
lab. A WAN covers a larger geographical area. The internet is the biggest
familiar example of interconnected networks on a global scale.
[[beat:protocols]] The textbook introduces several protocols. TCP/IP is
central to internet communication. UDP offers fast communication but without
the same reliability mechanisms. FTP is associated with transferring files,
and POP, post office protocol, is associated with retrieving email. Don't
try to memorize a paragraph for each one yet. In chapter one you just have
to remember: TCP/IP is internet communication, UDP is faster and less
reliable delivery, FTP is files, and POP is email retrieval.
