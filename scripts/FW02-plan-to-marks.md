[[beat:recap]] In the previous lesson, we learned how to decode a Functional
Writing question using four things. Audience. Purpose. Format. And Key
Instructions. Or, simply: A P F K. But understanding the question is only
half the battle. [[beat:goal]] Now comes the bigger challenge. How do you
turn those instructions into an answer that actually earns high marks?
Because simply mentioning every bullet point is not enough. In this lesson,
I will show you how to develop your ideas, organize your writing, control
your tone, improve your language, and check your work before you submit it.
[[beat:basic]] Let's begin with one of the biggest differences between an
average answer and a strong answer. Suppose the question asks: Explain why
students enjoyed the sports day. A student writes: Students enjoyed the
sports day because it was fun. Is that wrong? No. But is it strong? Not
really. The student has answered the question, but only at a very basic
level. [[beat:developed]] Now listen to this version. Students particularly
enjoyed the inter-house competitions because everyone had an opportunity to
participate rather than simply watch. The friendly rivalry also created an
energetic atmosphere and encouraged students to support their classmates.
That answer does much more. It develops the idea. [[beat:pee]] Here is a
simple method you can use. Point. Explain. Effect or example. Let's try it.
Point: The new computer room was useful. Now explain it. The new computer
room allowed students to research information more quickly. Now add the
effect. As a result, students were able to complete group projects more
efficiently. That is development. [[beat:three_q]] So whenever you make an
important point, ask yourself three questions. Why? How? And what happened
because of this? Those questions will help you produce stronger content.

[[beat:boxes]] Now let's talk about bullet points. Imagine the question
says: Write a report about a school trip. Include what students enjoyed,
problems during the trip, and suggestions for future trips. Think of those
three bullet points as three boxes. Before you finish your answer, all three
boxes must be checked. [[beat:unbalanced]] A very common mistake is spending
too much time on the first point because you have many ideas. Then you look
at the clock and realize you have only a few minutes left. So the second and
third points become rushed. Your answer becomes unbalanced. Try to divide
your time and your word count sensibly. Give each major part of the task
enough attention. [[beat:organization]] Next comes organization. Every
paragraph should have a job. Do not throw all your ideas into one giant
block of writing. For example, in a report, your first paragraph might
explain the purpose of the report. The second paragraph might discuss what
went well. The third might explain the problems. And the fourth might give
recommendations. Simple organization makes your writing easier to follow.
And when your writing is easier to follow, your ideas become more
convincing. [[beat:progression]] Your paragraphs should also move forward.
Listen to this. The library was crowded. There were many students. A lot of
people were inside. The library had too many students. What happened there?
The writer repeated the same idea four times. [[beat:progression2]] Now
listen to this version. The library was extremely crowded during lunch
breaks, leaving many students without a suitable place to work. Additional
seating or a booking system for study tables could help reduce this problem.
Much better. The paragraph moves from problem, to effect, to solution. That
is the kind of progression you want.

[[beat:register]] Now let's talk about register. Register means the level
and style of language you use. Your language must match your audience.
Suppose you are writing to the principal. This sounds appropriate: I would
recommend increasing the number of supervised study areas available after
school. Now compare that with: You guys seriously need to sort this out.
Clearly, the second version is not appropriate for a principal.
[[beat:appropriate]] But here is the important point. Good writing is not
always formal writing. Good writing is appropriate writing. If you are
writing to a close friend, extremely formal language can sound unnatural. So
always ask: Who am I writing to? And would I realistically speak to this
person in this way? [[beat:collapse]] Be careful not to change register
halfway through. For example: Dear Sir, I am writing to express my concern
regarding the recent changes to the school timetable. Excellent. But then
three paragraphs later, you write: Anyway, the whole thing is pretty
annoying. Now your tone has collapsed. Your writing should sound consistent
from beginning to end.

[[beat:vocab]] Now let's look at vocabulary. Some students believe
high-level vocabulary means using extremely difficult words. It does not.
Strong vocabulary means choosing the most precise word. For example: The
rain was very heavy. That sentence is fine. But you could write: The rain
hammered against the windows. More vivid. Instead of: He walked quickly into
the hall. You could write: He rushed into the hall. Instead of: The
committee looked carefully at the proposal. You could write: The committee
examined the proposal. A strong verb can often replace several weaker words.
[[beat:overblown]] But do not use complicated vocabulary that you cannot
control. Imagine writing: The cafeteria situation created a catastrophic
humanitarian predicament. What actually happened? The cafeteria ran out of
sandwiches. That is too much. You do not need dramatic vocabulary just to
sound intelligent. Sophisticated writing means precision and control. Not
exaggeration. [[beat:variety]] Sentence variety also matters. Listen to
this. The event started at nine. Students arrived. Teachers arrived. The
competition started. Everyone was excited. Grammatically, that is
acceptable. But it sounds robotic. Now listen to this. By nine o'clock,
students and teachers had filled the field. As the first competition began,
the atmosphere quickly became energetic. Same basic information. But the
second version flows much better. Try to use a mixture of short sentences
and longer sentences. Short sentences can also create emphasis. For example:
We cannot ignore this problem. Simple. Direct. Effective.

[[beat:tenses]] Now let's move to grammar. First, tenses. Suppose you are
describing an event that already happened. You might write: The students
arrived at nine o'clock. That is past simple. But if one event happened
before another, you may need the past perfect. For example: By the time the
teachers arrived, the students had already entered the hall. The words had
already entered show that this happened first. The important thing is
consistency. Do not jump randomly between past and present. [[beat:sva]]
Next, subject-verb agreement. Look at this sentence. The list of
recommendations are attached. What is the main subject? The list. Not
recommendations. So the correct sentence is: The list of recommendations is
attached. Another example: The group of students was waiting outside. The
main subject is group. Always find the real subject before choosing the
verb. [[beat:punctuation]] Now punctuation. You do not need to use
complicated punctuation in every sentence. But you do need control over full
stops, commas, apostrophes, quotation marks, and sentence boundaries. Be
especially careful with very long sentences. If you have written four lines
and still have not reached a full stop, ask yourself: Do I need to end this
sentence? Sometimes the best punctuation correction is simply a full stop.
[[beat:conjunctions]] You may also have heard this rule: Never begin a
sentence with And, But, or So. That is not an absolute rule of English.
Writers can begin sentences with conjunctions. But in formal Functional
Writing, repeatedly beginning sentences with And, But, or So can make your
writing sound too conversational. The better rule is: Use them deliberately.
Do not use them automatically. [[beat:contractions]] What about
contractions? Words like: Don't. Can't. Won't. These are perfectly normal in
English. They may work well in an informal letter, a speech, or dialogue.
But in a formal report or formal letter, you may prefer: Do not. Cannot.
Will not. Again, the key idea is register. Use language that fits the
situation. [[beat:word_limits]] Now let's talk about word limits. Some
students think: If three hundred words are good, five hundred words must be
even better. No. The examiner is not paying you by the word. Writing too
much can create several problems. You waste time. You repeat yourself. And
every extra sentence gives you another chance to make a spelling, grammar,
or punctuation mistake. So follow the word range given in your examination.
Your goal is not to write the most. Your goal is to write the most
effectively.

[[beat:checking]] Now we come to one of the easiest ways to save marks.
Checking your work. Do not finish your answer and immediately stop. Leave
time to check. But do not just reread the whole answer casually. Use a
system. First, check content. Did I cover every bullet point? Actually look
back at the question. Do not trust your memory. [[beat:check2]] Second,
check audience and register. Does my language sound appropriate for the
person I am writing to? [[beat:check3]] Third, check tenses. Are my verbs
consistent? Did I accidentally write something like: Yesterday we go to the
museum? If so, fix it. [[beat:check4]] Fourth, check subject-verb agreement.
Look carefully at sentences containing phrases like: The group of. The
number of. The list of. Make sure the verb agrees with the true subject.
[[beat:check5]] Fifth, check punctuation. Look for missing full stops.
Missing commas. Apostrophe errors. And dialogue punctuation if you used
dialogue. [[beat:check6]] Finally, check for missing words. This is
important. Your brain knows what you intended to write. So if you
accidentally write: The students very excited about the competition. Your
brain may silently insert the missing word were. Read carefully enough to
see what is actually on the page.

[[beat:together]] Now let's put everything together. Before writing,
remember: Audience. Purpose. Format. Key Instructions. A P F K. While
writing: Cover every point. Develop your ideas. Organize clearly. Maintain
the correct register. Use precise language. And after writing: Check your
work. [[beat:control]] Functional Writing is not about showing the examiner
every difficult word you know. It is about control. Can you control your
content? Can you control your tone? Can you control your structure? Can you
control your grammar? And can you do all of that while following the task
exactly? If the answer is yes, you are no longer simply writing an answer.
You are writing for marks.
