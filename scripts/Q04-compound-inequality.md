[[beat:hook]] Can a cricket player with a score of eighty-four still finish
with a rating of ninety or higher? Let's find out. [[beat:setup]] Coach
Malik is selecting players for an elite cricket squad. Each player's final
rating is based on two things. Forty percent comes from practice
performance. And sixty percent comes from a final batting trial.
[[beat:ali]] Ali has already scored eighty-four in practice. Let's call his
batting trial score x. So his final rating is: zero point four times
eighty-four, plus zero point six times x. [[beat:target]] Now Ali wants a
final rating between ninety and one hundred. So we write: ninety is less
than or equal to zero point four times eighty-four plus zero point six x,
which is less than or equal to one hundred. [[beat:compound]] This is called
a compound inequality. Why? Because Ali's score has both a minimum and a
maximum. [[beat:step1]] Now let's solve it. Forty percent of eighty-four is
thirty-three point six. So now we have: ninety is less than or equal to
thirty-three point six plus zero point six x, which is less than or equal to
one hundred. [[beat:step2]] Subtract thirty-three point six from all three
parts. That gives us: fifty-six point four is less than or equal to zero
point six x, which is less than or equal to sixty-six point four.
[[beat:step3]] Now divide everything by zero point six. We get: ninety-four
is less than or equal to x, which is less than or equal to about one hundred
ten point seven. [[beat:catch]] But there's a catch. The batting trial is
scored out of one hundred. So the real range is: ninety-four to one hundred.
[[beat:answer]] That means Ali needs at least ninety-four on the final
batting trial to reach the top rating. [[beat:key]] And here's the key idea.
Whenever a problem gives you a minimum and a maximum, think compound
inequality. [[beat:boundaries]] Just like cricket boundaries, your answer
has to stay inside the limits.
