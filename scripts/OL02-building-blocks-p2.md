[[beat:ordering]] Part 3: Comparing and Ordering Numbers. Now let's move to
ordering. Ordering simply means deciding: Which number is bigger? Which
number is smaller? And where do these numbers belong in relation to one
another? We use symbols like: greater than, less than, and not equal to.
[[beat:open_side]] You'll sometimes hear students say they can't remember
which way the greater-than sign points. Here's the simple idea. The open
side faces the larger number. So: 12 is greater than 10. The wider opening
points toward twelve. [[beat:data_package]] Let's use a familiar example.
Suppose one mobile package gives you ten gigabytes. Another gives you twelve
gigabytes. Same price. Which is larger? Twelve. So: 12 is greater than 10.
Easy. [[beat:messy]] But in the exam, they're not always going to give you
neat whole numbers. They may give you: fractions, decimals, negative
numbers, percentages, or a mixture. Now you need a strategy.
[[beat:compare_convert]] Suppose I ask you to compare: three-quarters and
zero point eight. Which is bigger? Don't guess. Convert them into the same
form. Three-quarters is zero point seven five. So we're comparing: zero
point seven five and zero point eight. Now it's obvious. Zero point eight is
larger. [[beat:same_language]] Or perhaps keep everything as fractions.
Whatever makes the comparison easier. The rule is: put numbers into the same
language. If I'm comparing decimals and fractions, I may convert one. If I'm
comparing fractions with different denominators, I may use a common
denominator. [[beat:negatives]] Now negative numbers can be interesting.
Which is larger? Minus two or minus seven? Some students see seven and
immediately think minus seven is bigger. But picture the number line. Minus
seven is further to the left. Minus two is closer to zero. So minus two is
greater than minus seven. [[beat:temperature]] Think about temperature.
Would you rather be standing outside at minus two degrees or minus seven
degrees? Minus two is warmer. It's the greater number. [[beat:neg_rule]] So
when ordering negative numbers, be careful. The number with the larger
absolute value can actually be the smaller number. Minus ten is less than
minus three. [[beat:number_sense]] Again, this is why number sense matters.
You shouldn't just be manipulating symbols. You should have a feeling for
where numbers live.

[[beat:prime_intro]] Part 4: Prime Factorisation. Now let's finish with one
of the most useful ideas in this lesson. Prime factorisation. I like to
think of prime factors as the DNA of a number. You take a number... break it
down... break it down again... and eventually you're left with only prime
numbers. Those prime numbers are the building blocks. [[beat:tree36]] Let's
take 36. We can build a factor tree. Start with any two factors. Maybe: 2
times 18. Two is already prime. So we're done with that branch. Now break
down 18. Two times nine. Again, two is prime. Now break nine. Three times
three. Both primes. So we've reached the bottom. [[beat:tree_result]] Now
list all the primes: 2 times 2 times 3 times 3. We can write that more
neatly as: 2 squared times 3 squared. That is the prime factorisation of 36.
[[beat:routes]] Now here's an important point. You could have started with:
four times nine. Then broken four into two times two. And nine into three
times three. You still end up with: 2 squared times 3 squared. That's the
beauty of prime factorisation. You can take different routes... but the
prime building blocks are the same. [[beat:why_care]] Now why do we care?
Because prime factorisation helps us solve problems involving: HCF, Highest
Common Factor, and LCM, Lowest Common Multiple. [[beat:hcf]] Let's make HCF
simple. Suppose I have two numbers. Twelve and eighteen. Let's prime
factorise them. Twelve is: 2 squared times 3. Eighteen is: 2 times 3
squared. For the HCF, we want the factors they share. Both have a two. Both
have a three. So the HCF is: 2 times 3, which is 6. And yes, six is the
largest number that divides exactly into both twelve and eighteen.
[[beat:lcm_buses]] LCM asks something different. What's the smallest
multiple that both numbers eventually reach? Let's use a real-world example.
Imagine two buses leave a terminal at the same time. Bus A leaves every 40
minutes. Bus B leaves every 15 minutes. They both leave at 10:00 in the
morning. When will they next leave together? We're looking for the smallest
number of minutes that is a multiple of both 40 and 15. That is the LCM.
[[beat:lcm_calc]] Let's calculate it. Prime factorisation of 40: 2 cubed
times 5. Prime factorisation of 15: 3 times 5. For the LCM, we need enough
prime factors to build both numbers. So we take: 2 cubed, times 3, times 5.
That's: 8 times 3 times 5. 24 times 5. 120. So 120 minutes. That's two
hours. If both buses leave together at 10:00... they next leave together at
12:00. [[beat:recognition]] This is a very typical example of how something
that looks like a number theory question becomes a practical scheduling
problem. And that's something I want you to start noticing in O Level
Mathematics. The exam may not tell you: Find the LCM. It might give you
buses. Lights flashing. Machines operating. Events repeating. And your job
is to recognize: Ah. This is an LCM problem. That recognition is what
separates learning a technique from actually being able to use it.

[[beat:review]] Quick Review. Alright. Before we finish, let's see what you
remember. Natural numbers? Counting numbers starting from one. Integers?
Positive and negative whole numbers, including zero. Prime number? Exactly
two positive factors: one and itself. Is one prime? No. Only even prime
number? Two. Rational number? Can be written as a fraction. Irrational?
Cannot be expressed as a ratio of integers and has a decimal expansion that
continues without a repeating pattern. [[beat:review_bodmas]] BODMAS?
Brackets. Orders. Division and multiplication. Addition and subtraction. And
remember: division and multiplication are equal priority. Addition and
subtraction are equal priority. Work left to right when they appear at the
same level. [[beat:review_compare]] When comparing fractions and decimals?
Put them into the same form. And prime factorisation? Break the number down
until every factor is prime. [[beat:homework]] Excellent. Now here's what I
want you to do after this lesson. Don't just watch the video and think: Yes,
I understood everything. That feels good. But it doesn't tell me whether you
can actually do the mathematics. Take five numbers. Let's say: 24, 36, 45,
60, and 72. Prime factorise each one. Then choose any two and find their HCF
and LCM. Next, create five BODMAS questions for yourself. Actually write
them down. Then solve them without a calculator. And finally, take a few
fractions and decimals and practice ordering them from smallest to largest.
[[beat:paper1]] Remember: Paper 1 does not care how friendly your calculator
normally is. On that paper, the calculator stays away. So we need to build
fluency from day one. [[beat:closing]] And that's really what this first
lesson is about. Not difficult mathematics. Strong foundations. Because if
your number skills are strong, algebra gets easier. Percentages get easier.
Ratio gets easier. Probability gets easier. Almost everything becomes
easier. So don't rush past the basics. Master them. We'll build on them in
the next lesson. Thank you for joining me. Practice the questions. Don't
just watch. And I'll see you in the next session. Allah Hafiz.
