[[beat:hook]] One tiny word can destroy the timeline of an entire sentence.
A paragraph may begin in the past. Everything may seem perfectly clear. Then
one verb suddenly jumps into the present. [[beat:watch]] It is like watching
a historical movie set in the eighteen hundreds and noticing an actor
wearing a digital watch. The costume may look correct. The buildings may
look correct. But that one modern object ruins the entire scene.
[[beat:promise]] By the end of this lesson, you will know how to keep verbs
in the correct timeline. You will also learn when changing tense is not only
acceptable, but necessary. And near the end, I will give you a sentence
containing a hidden time traveler. See whether you can catch it before I
reveal the answer. [[beat:title]] Let us begin. Verb tense tells us when an
action happens. It may happen in the past. It may be happening now. Or it
may happen in the future. [[beat:maria]] For example: Maria walked to the
library. That action happened in the past. Maria walks to the library. That
action happens regularly in the present. Maria will walk to the library.
That action will happen in the future. [[beat:consistent]] Usually, choosing
the tense is not difficult. The real challenge is keeping the tense
consistent.

[[beat:movie]] Imagine that you are directing a movie. Every paragraph is a
scene. When a scene begins in the past, the actions inside that scene will
usually remain in the past. When a scene begins in the present, the actions
will usually remain in the present. [[beat:scientist]] Consider this
sentence: The scientist entered the laboratory, examines the samples, and
recorded the results. Something feels wrong. The first verb is entered. That
is past tense. The final verb is recorded. That is also past tense. But the
middle verb, examines, is present tense. It has walked into our historical
scene wearing a digital watch. [[beat:scientist_fix]] To repair the
sentence, we need to keep all three actions in the same timeline. The
scientist entered the laboratory, examined the samples, and recorded the
results. Entered. Examined. Recorded. All three verbs remain in the past.
The scene is consistent. [[beat:plant]] Now listen to this example: The
plant belongs to a tropical family, grows in warm climates, and produces
bright flowers. The verbs are belongs, grows, and produces. They are all in
the present tense. The sentence describes facts that are generally true.
Therefore, the present tense is appropriate. [[beat:plant_wrong]] If we
suddenly wrote: The plant belongs to a tropical family, grew in warm
climates, and produces bright flowers, the word grew would interrupt the
scene. Unless there is a clear reason to discuss the past, grew should
remain grows. [[beat:strategy1]] This gives us our first strategy. Look at
the surrounding verbs. They establish the timeline of the paragraph. If the
nearby verbs are is, belongs, grows, and produces, the missing or underlined
verb will probably need to be in the present tense. If the surrounding verbs
are was, belonged, grew, and produced, the missing verb will probably need
to be in the past tense. Do not look at one verb in isolation. Look at the
entire movie scene.

[[beat:shift]] However, tense consistency does not mean that every verb in a
paragraph must always use exactly the same tense. Sometimes, the story
genuinely moves from one time period to another. In that situation, changing
tense is necessary. [[beat:past_perfect]] Imagine a movie scene showing a
woman receiving an award in nineteen ninety. The narrator then explains
something she had accomplished many years earlier. We now have two events in
the past. But one event happened before the other. To make the order clear,
we can use the past perfect tense. The past perfect is formed with had plus
the past participle of the verb. [[beat:martha]] For example: By the time
Martha retired, she had given hundreds of performances. There are two past
actions. First, she gave hundreds of performances. Later, she retired. The
performances happened before the retirement. So we say: She had given
hundreds of performances. The word had sends us further back into the past.
It acts like a time machine. [[beat:firefighters]] The phrase by the time is
often an important signal. Listen again: By the time the firefighters
arrived, the residents had left the building. Which happened first? The
residents left. Which happened later? The firefighters arrived. Because
leaving happened before another past event, we use had left.
[[beat:meeting]] Here is another example: By the time the meeting began, the
manager had reviewed the proposal. First, the manager reviewed the proposal.
Later, the meeting began. The phrase had reviewed makes the sequence clear.
[[beat:pp_caution]] But be careful. Do not use the past perfect simply
because a sentence is in the past. Use it when you need to show that one
past action happened before another past action.

[[beat:hypo]] Now let us discuss hypothetical situations. A hypothetical
situation is imagined. It could happen, but it has not happened yet. In
these situations, we often use the word would. For example: If Maya had more
time, she would learn another language. Maya does not currently have more
time. We are imagining what could happen. [[beat:equipment]] Here is another
example: With better equipment, the team would complete the project more
quickly. The sentence describes a possible result, not a confirmed event.
The word would helps show that the situation is hypothetical.
[[beat:would_past]] Would can also describe the future from a past point of
view. For example: In nineteen ninety-five, the young inventor did not know
that her discovery would transform the industry. The transformation happened
after nineteen ninety-five. But the sentence is being told from a point in
the past. Therefore, we use would transform rather than will transform.

[[beat:traveler]] Now it is time for the hidden time traveler. Listen
carefully. Last summer, Daniel traveled across the country, visited several
national parks, and writes about the experience in his journal.
[[beat:reveal]] Which verb does not belong? Traveled is past tense. Visited
is past tense. But writes is present tense. Writes is the actor wearing the
digital watch. [[beat:traveler_fix]] The correct sentence is: Last summer,
Daniel traveled across the country, visited several national parks, and
wrote about the experience in his journal. Traveled. Visited. Wrote. All
three actions belong in the same past-tense scene.

[[beat:method]] Here is the method to remember. First, identify the
timeline. Is the sentence discussing the past, the present, or the future?
Second, examine the surrounding verbs. They usually reveal which tense the
sentence needs. Third, look for a genuine reason to change time. A phrase
such as by the time may signal that one past event happened before another.
In that case, use had plus the past participle. Finally, look for
hypothetical situations. When the result is imagined rather than certain,
would may be the correct choice. [[beat:director]] Think of every paragraph
as a movie scene. Keep the costumes accurate. Keep the setting consistent.
And most importantly, do not allow a verb from another time period to wander
into the background. Once you learn to recognize the timeline, verb tense
becomes much easier. You are no longer guessing which verb sounds right. You
are directing the scene. [[beat:outro]] So that was our Verb Tense and the
Movie Scene Rule lecture. See you soon.
