[[beat:hook]] Assalam-o-Alaikum, everyone. Welcome to our first proper O
Level Mathematics lesson. Now before we start, let me ask you something.
Have you ever looked at a mathematics question and thought: I know how to do
this, but somehow I still got the wrong answer? [[beat:small_mistakes]] That
happens a lot. And very often, the problem is not some difficult formula. It
is something basic. A negative sign. The order of operations. A fraction. A
prime factor. One small mistake at the beginning, and the entire answer
falls apart. [[beat:foundation]] So today we're going to work on the
foundation. And I want you to think of mathematics like building a house. If
the foundation is weak, it doesn't really matter how beautiful the top floor
is. Eventually, something is going to crack. [[beat:agenda]] Today we're
covering four things: Types of numbers. Order of operations. Comparing and
ordering numbers. And prime factorisation. Now, none of these topics sounds
frightening. Good. Because I don't want today's lesson to be frightening.
But don't confuse basic with unimportant. These ideas show up everywhere in
O Level Mathematics. And especially on Paper 1, where you do not have a
calculator sitting there to rescue you. So let's get started.

[[beat:families]] Part 1: Types of numbers. Let's begin with something very
simple. Numbers are not all the same. We put them into different families.
Just like people can belong to different groups, numbers can belong to
different groups. And one number can sometimes belong to more than one
group. [[beat:natural]] Let's start with the easiest one. Natural numbers.
Natural numbers are basically your counting numbers. One. Two. Three. Four.
Five. And so on. Imagine you're sitting with four friends at a chai place.
You count: One cup. Two cups. Three cups. Four cups. Those are natural
numbers. You're not going to tell the waiter: Brother, please bring me
negative two cups of chai. That doesn't make any sense. So natural numbers
are the numbers we naturally use when counting things. One, two, three,
four, five, and so on. Easy. [[beat:integers]] Now let's expand the family.
Integers. Integers include the positive whole numbers, zero, and the
negative whole numbers. So: minus three. Minus two. Minus one. Zero. One.
Two. Three. All of those are integers. [[beat:integers_ex]] Now where do
negative numbers actually show up? Lots of places. Temperature is an easy
example. If you're in Lahore in June, you're probably not worrying about
minus five degrees. But imagine you're somewhere much colder. Quetta.
Ziarat. Or you're looking at the weather in northern Europe. The temperature
could be minus five degrees Celsius. That minus five is an integer. Bank
accounts are another example. Suppose you have 2,000 rupees. That's
positive. But suppose somehow you owe the bank 2,000 rupees. Now we're
thinking in the negative direction. So integers help us represent quantities
that can go above and below zero. [[beat:int_check]] Now a quick question.
Is 4.5 an integer? No. Why not? Because integers are whole numbers. No
decimal part. How about negative seven? Yes. That's an integer. How about
zero? Yes. Zero is also an integer. Students sometimes forget that.

[[beat:primes]] Now let's move to a very important group. Prime numbers.
Prime numbers are special. A prime number has exactly two positive factors.
One, and itself. That's it. For example: Two. What divides exactly into two?
One and two. So two is prime. Three? One and three. Prime. Five? One and
five. Prime. Seven? One and seven. Prime. Now what about six? One divides
six. Two divides six. Three divides six. Six divides six. Too many factors.
So six is not prime. [[beat:one_trap]] Here's an exam trap. What about the
number one? Is one prime? No. And I want you to remember this. One is not a
prime number. Why? Because a prime number must have exactly two factors. But
one only has one factor. One. So it doesn't qualify. [[beat:two_even]] Now
another interesting point. Two is the only even prime number. Why? Because
every other even number is divisible by two. So once an even number is
greater than two, it automatically has at least three factors: one, two, and
itself. So two is special. The only even prime. [[beat:prime_check]] Let's
do a quick mental check. Is 11 prime? Yes. 13? Yes. 15? No. Why? Because
three times five is fifteen. 17? Prime. 21? Not prime. Three times seven.
Good.

[[beat:rational]] Now let's move into a distinction that becomes very
important later. Rational and irrational numbers. The word rational sounds
complicated. The idea is not. A rational number is a number that can be
written as a fraction. A fraction of integers. For example: one-half.
Three-quarters. Five. Yes, even five is rational. Why? Because I can write
five as five over one. Zero point five is rational. Because zero point five
is one-half. Zero point seven five is rational. Because that's
three-quarters. Even some repeating decimals are rational. For example: zero
point three three three three, continuing forever. That can be written as
one-third. So it is rational. [[beat:irrational]] Now irrational numbers are
different. These are numbers that cannot be written neatly as a fraction of
two integers. Their decimal expansions continue forever without settling
into a repeating pattern. Two famous examples are: pi. And the square root
of two. Pi starts: three point one four one five nine, and keeps going. The
square root of two is approximately: one point four one four two, and also
keeps going without repeating in a fixed pattern. So these are irrational
numbers. [[beat:ratio_memory]] Here's a useful way to remember it. Rational
numbers can be expressed as a ratio. That's where the word rational comes
from. Ratio. Fraction. Rational. [[beat:overlap]] Now don't panic about all
these classifications. The main thing is to understand what kind of number
you're dealing with. Natural number. Integer. Prime. Rational. Irrational.
And remember that categories can overlap. For example, the number five is:
natural, an integer, prime, and rational. One number. Several
classifications.

[[beat:bodmas]] Part 2: BODMAS. Now we move to something that causes an
amazing number of unnecessary mistakes. Order of operations. Or what many of
you know as: BODMAS. Some textbooks call it BIDMAS. Same basic idea. BODMAS
tells us the order in which operations must be performed. B stands for
brackets. O stands for orders. Things like powers and roots. D is division.
M is multiplication. A is addition. S is subtraction. [[beat:why_rule]] Now
why do we need a rule? Because without one, the same calculation could
produce different answers depending on who does it. Mathematics cannot work
like that. We need one agreed system. [[beat:samosa]] Let's take a simple
everyday example. Suppose you're buying snacks for a cricket match. You buy
five samosas. Each samosa costs 30 rupees. And you've got a 50-rupee
discount. So what is the bill? Five times thirty, minus fifty. Five times
thirty is one hundred and fifty. Minus fifty. Your total is one hundred
rupees. Fine. But imagine somebody incorrectly puts the thirty minus fifty
together first. Now you've got: five times negative twenty. Negative one
hundred rupees. So apparently the shopkeeper now owes you money for buying
samosas. Wonderful business model. Unfortunately, that's not how it works.
[[beat:example1]] The order matters. Let's take a more exam-style example.
Work out: 12 plus 8 divided by open bracket 9 minus 5 close bracket. Don't
rush. What comes first? Bracket. Nine minus five is four. So now we have: 12
plus 8 divided by 4. What comes next? Division. Eight divided by four is
two. So now: 12 plus 2. Answer? 14. Simple. But if you ignore the order and
just start calculating from left to right, you can very easily get the wrong
answer. [[beat:example2]] Let's try another one. Suppose we have: 3 plus 4
times 5. What's the answer? If you simply go left to right: three plus four
is seven. Seven times five is thirty-five. Wrong. BODMAS tells us
multiplication comes before addition. So: four times five is twenty. Then:
three plus twenty. Twenty-three. That's the correct answer.
[[beat:equal_priority]] Now here's something students get confused about.
Division and multiplication have equal priority. Addition and subtraction
also have equal priority. So if multiplication and division appear together,
you work from left to right. Same thing with addition and subtraction. For
example: 24 divided by 6 times 2. We do not automatically say multiplication
comes after division because D appears before M in the word BODMAS. They are
equal priority. Go left to right. Twenty-four divided by six is four. Four
times two is eight. Answer: eight. [[beat:powers]] Let's do one with powers.
Two plus three squared times four. What happens first? The order. Three
squared is nine. So now we have: two plus nine times four. Multiplication
next. Nine times four is thirty-six. Then add two. Thirty-eight. That's
BODMAS. [[beat:control]] And students, this is exactly the kind of thing
that matters in a non-calculator paper. You need control. You should not be
guessing which operation to perform next. It should become automatic.
