[[beat:hook]] Most people make subject-verb agreement mistakes for one
surprising reason. They match the verb with the noun standing closest to it.
And that noun is often a trap. [[beat:promise]] By the end of this lesson,
you will know how to find the real subject in even a long, confusing
sentence. You will also learn one simple trick that can help you eliminate
the wrong verb almost immediately. [[beat:challenge]] Later, I will give you
a sentence that looks completely correct to most people, but contains a
hidden agreement error. See whether you can catch it before I reveal the
answer. [[beat:title]] Let us begin with a lonely traveler, a tiny pet, and
one of the most useful rules in English grammar. Today, we are learning
subject-verb agreement.

[[beat:traveler]] Subject-verb agreement. That name may sound serious. But
the idea is actually simple. Imagine a traveler walking alone down a long
road. This traveler is the subject of our sentence. Because the traveler is
alone, the traveler wants a little pet for company. That pet is the letter
S. [[beat:rule]] So, when the subject is one person, one place, one object,
or one idea, the verb often receives the letter S. [[beat:researcher]] For
example: The researcher believes the evidence. The researcher is one person.
The researcher is traveling alone. So the verb believe gets its little pet.
Believe becomes believes. [[beat:researchers]] Now imagine that several
researchers arrive. They are no longer lonely. They have one another for
company. Because the subject is plural, the verb does not need the letter S.
The researchers believe the evidence. Listen to the difference. The
researcher believes. The researchers believe. One researcher gets believes.
Several researchers get believe. [[beat:lonely_rule]] That is the Lonely
Subject Rule. In the simple present tense, a third-person singular subject
usually takes a verb ending in S. A plural subject usually takes the base
form of the verb, without the extra S. [[beat:dog]] Let us try another
example. The dog runs through the park. There is one dog. The dog is lonely.
So the verb gets the letter S. The dog runs. [[beat:dogs]] Now let us invite
some more dogs. The dogs run through the park. The dogs are a group. They
are not lonely. So the verb does not get the extra S. One dog runs. Several
dogs run.

[[beat:bodyguard]] This sounds easy. Unfortunately, sentences do not always
allow the subject and the verb to stand next to each other. Sometimes, the
sentence hires a bodyguard. The bodyguard stands between the subject and the
verb. Its job is to distract you. [[beat:woodpecker]] Consider this
sentence. The feathers of the woodpecker has evolved over time. At first,
you may notice the word woodpecker. A woodpecker is singular. So you might
think that has is correct. But the woodpecker is not the real subject. The
real subject is feathers. What has evolved? The woodpecker? No. The feathers
have evolved. [[beat:woodpecker2]] The phrase of the woodpecker is only
giving us more information about the feathers. It is the bodyguard phrase.
Mentally remove it. The feathers have evolved over time. Now the answer
becomes clear. Feathers is plural. Therefore, we need have, not has.
[[beat:box]] Let us examine another sentence. The box of old photographs
sits in the attic. What is the subject? Is it photographs? No. The sentence
is not saying that the photographs sit in the attic. It is saying that the
box sits in the attic. The phrase of old photographs is the bodyguard.
Remove it for a moment. The box sits in the attic. Box is singular. So sit
receives the letter S. Sits. [[beat:players]] Now listen to this example.
The players on the field runs every morning. The word field is singular. But
field is hiding inside the bodyguard phrase, on the field. Remove that
phrase. The players runs every morning. That sounds wrong. Players is
plural. So the correct sentence is: The players on the field run every
morning.

[[beat:strategy]] Here is a strategy you can use whenever a sentence feels
confusing. First, find the verb. Next, ask this question: Who or what is
performing the action? That word is probably your subject. Then ignore any
distracting phrase between the subject and the verb. [[beat:preps]] Phrases
beginning with words such as of, in, on, by, with, and near often act as
bodyguards. They provide extra information, but they do not control the
verb. [[beat:coins]] Let us practice. The collection of rare coins belongs
to the museum. What belongs to the museum? The coins? No. The collection
belongs to the museum. Collection is singular. Therefore, belongs is
correct. [[beat:effects]] Now try this one. The effects of the new policy
concerns many employees. What concerns the employees? The policy? No. The
effects concern the employees. Effects is plural. So the correct verb is
concern, without the letter S.

[[beat:habit]] Here is the most important habit to remember. Do not
automatically match the verb with the closest noun. The closest noun may be
a bodyguard. Instead, locate the true subject. Ask who or what is actually
doing the action. Then use the Lonely Subject Rule. One subject usually gets
a verb with S. More than one subject usually gets a verb without S.
[[beat:pairs]] The bird sings. The birds sing. The teacher explains. The
teachers explain. The machine works. The machines work. [[beat:closing]] And
whenever a distracting phrase appears, calmly move past the bodyguard. Find
the real subject. Decide whether it is lonely or part of a group. Then
choose the verb that agrees with it. Once you learn to spot the true
subject, even long sentences become much easier. The bodyguards may look
intimidating. But now, you know exactly how to get past them.
