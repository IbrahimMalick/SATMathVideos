scene: concept.functional_writing

[[beat:hook]] Imagine this. You write a beautiful answer. Your vocabulary is
excellent. Your grammar is strong. Your handwriting is neat. And then you
lose marks. Why? Because you answered the wrong task. That is one of the
biggest mistakes students make in Functional Writing, also called Directed
Writing. [[beat:apfk]] In this lesson, I am going to show you a simple
four-part system that helps you understand exactly what the examiner wants
before you write a single sentence. Remember these four letters: A P F K.
Audience. Purpose. Format. Key Instructions. If you master these four
things, you dramatically reduce the chances of throwing away easy marks.
[[beat:what_is]] Functional Writing is different from simply being asked to
write something interesting. You are being given a communication task. The
examiner wants to know: Can you understand a situation? Can you identify who
you are speaking to? Can you communicate for the correct purpose? Can you
use the correct format? And most importantly: Can you follow instructions?
[[beat:forms]] Think of Functional Writing as real-life communication. You
might be asked to write: a letter, a report, a speech, an article, an
account, or another practical form of writing. The language you use depends
on the situation. So never rush directly into the answer. First, decode the
task.

[[beat:audience]] A. Audience. The first question is: Who am I writing to?
This matters because your audience controls your tone. [[beat:cafeteria]]
Imagine you want to complain about the school cafeteria. If you are speaking
to your friend, you might say: The food was terrible today. I couldn't even
finish it. But if you are writing to your principal, you might say: I would
like to raise a concern regarding the quality of food currently being served
in the cafeteria. Same basic message. Completely different tone. Why?
Because the audience changed. [[beat:audience_list]] Your audience might be:
a principal, a teacher, a friend, parents, students, newspaper readers,
members of the local community. So before you write, ask: Who is going to
read this? Then ask: How would I realistically speak to this person? That
gives you your register. [[beat:register]] What is register? Register simply
means the level and style of language you use. Formal writing may include
expressions such as: I would like to suggest. It has come to my attention. I
strongly recommend. Informal writing might include: You won't believe what
happened. I had an amazing time. The best part was. [[beat:register_rule]]
Neither is automatically better. The important thing is to use the right
register for the right audience. A formal report should not suddenly sound
like a WhatsApp message. And a letter to a close friend should not sound
like a government document.

[[beat:purpose]] P. Purpose. Next, ask: Why am I writing? What is the
examiner asking you to accomplish? Are you trying to: inform? persuade?
complain? recommend? describe? explain? give an account? evaluate ideas?
Purpose controls the type of language you need. [[beat:persuade_ex]] Suppose
you are asked to persuade students to join a school environmental campaign.
You don't simply give information. You need to make them want to
participate. You might use: Imagine arriving at school every morning to a
cleaner, greener campus. Or: If every student gave just thirty minutes a
week, the difference could be remarkable. [[beat:report_ex]] Now imagine the
purpose is simply to report on the campaign. You would be more factual: The
campaign began on Monday and involved approximately 80 students. Different
purpose. Different language. [[beat:one_sentence]] The one-sentence test.
Here is a very useful trick. Before writing, complete this sentence: By the
end of my writing, I want the reader to. For example: understand what
happened. Agree with my recommendation. Take action. Understand why I am
dissatisfied. Consider both sides of the issue. If you cannot complete that
sentence clearly, you probably do not yet understand the purpose of the
task.

[[beat:format]] F. Format. Now ask: What exactly am I being asked to write?
A letter? A report? A speech? An article? This sounds obvious, but students
sometimes become so focused on the topic that they forget the format.
[[beat:format_wrong]] Suppose the question asks: Write a speech to your
classmates. And you begin: To: The Principal. From: Student Council.
Subject: School Facilities. You have written something that looks like a
report. The topic might be correct. But the format is wrong.
[[beat:letters]] Letters. A letter should sound as though one person is
communicating directly with another. The tone depends on who the recipient
is. A formal letter might begin: Dear Sir or Madam, or with a named
recipient. An informal letter might begin: Dear Ahmed. The exact layout can
depend on your syllabus, so follow the conventions you have been taught. But
above all, make sure the voice sounds appropriate for a letter.
[[beat:reports]] Reports. Reports are usually more: organized, factual,
direct, objective. A useful opening technique is the 4 Ws: Who? What? Where?
When? For example: On 15 March, members of the Student Council inspected the
school library to assess its facilities and identify areas requiring
improvement. Who? Student Council. What? An inspection. Where? The school
library. When? 15 March. The reader understands the situation immediately.
[[beat:speeches]] Speeches. A speech needs to sound spoken. You should
acknowledge your audience. For example: Good morning, teachers and fellow
students. Then make your purpose clear. I would like to speak today about an
issue that affects every student in our school. A speech may also use:
rhetorical questions, repetition, direct address, persuasive language. For
example: How many of us have complained about litter around the school? That
sounds like something a person could actually say to an audience.

[[beat:key]] K. Key Instructions. Now we reach perhaps the most important
part. The question will usually tell you exactly what content to include.
These instructions may appear as bullet points. For example: Write a report
about a recent school event. Include: what went well, problems that
occurred, recommendations for next year. [[beat:roadmap]] Those three points
are not suggestions. They are your marking roadmap. If you write two pages
about what went well but forget the problems and recommendations, you will
lose content marks. No matter how beautiful your English is. So underline
the instructions. Circle them. Number them. Whatever method works for you.
Just make sure that every instruction appears somewhere in your answer.
[[beat:plan]] Turn instructions into a plan. Suppose the question gives you
three bullet points: Explain why the event was successful. Describe problems
that occurred. Suggest improvements. Before writing, make a quick plan:
Paragraph 1: Purpose and context. Paragraph 2: Successes. Paragraph 3:
Problems. Paragraph 4: Recommendations. Now the structure of your answer is
already visible. You have reduced the chance of forgetting anything.
[[beat:command]] Watch the command words. Pay attention to verbs in the
question. There is a difference between describe and explain. If the
question says: Describe the problem, you tell the reader what happened. If
it says: Explain why the problem occurred, you need reasons. If it says:
Evaluate the suggestions, you need to consider strengths, weaknesses, or
effectiveness. Do not treat every command word as though it simply means
mention. [[beat:scenario]] Stay inside the scenario. Another common mistake
is changing the facts. Suppose the reading text says: The event was held on
Saturday and 150 students attended. Do not write: On Friday, more than 500
students arrived. You can develop ideas. You can explain consequences. You
can infer reasonable details if the task allows it. But do not contradict
important facts provided in the question or source material. Think of those
facts as the boundaries of the task.

[[beat:routine]] Your sixty-second planning routine. Before you start
writing, spend approximately one minute asking: A. Audience. Who am I
writing to? P. Purpose. What am I trying to achieve? F. Format. What type of
writing is required? K. Key Instructions. What must I include? Then make a
short paragraph plan. That single minute can save you from losing several
marks later. [[beat:challenge]] Final challenge. Let me give you a mini
question. Your school is considering extending the school day. Write a
speech to your classmates explaining the proposal, giving your opinion, and
suggesting an alternative. [[beat:challenge_answer]] Before writing
anything, identify: Audience? Your classmates. Purpose? To explain, give
your opinion, and suggest an alternative. Format? A speech. Key
instructions? Three: Explain the proposal. Give your opinion. Suggest an
alternative. That is APFK in action. [[beat:conclusion]] Functional Writing
does not begin with writing. It begins with reading intelligently. Before
you write, remember: A P F K. Audience. Purpose. Format. Key Instructions.
Understand those four things first. Then write. Because the examiner cannot
reward you for answering the question you thought you were being asked. Your
job is to answer the question that is actually on the page.
