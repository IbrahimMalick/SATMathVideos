[[beat:hook]] If computer science feels like ten chapters, hundreds of
definitions and a confusing amount of programming, give me the next forty
minutes. [[beat:promise]] Because by the end of this lesson, I'm going to do
something that could save you months of studying the wrong way. I'm going to
take the entire Cambridge O Level Computer Science course and turn it into a
map you can actually understand. [[beat:one_map]] The entire course. One
map. [[beat:you_will_know]] When we finish, you'll know what you actually
need to learn, why Paper 1 and Paper 2 require different skills, where
students commonly lose marks, how programming fits into this examination,
and most importantly, the order in which you should learn everything.
[[beat:scattered]] So when somebody says binary, packets, CPU registers,
phishing, algorithms, pseudocode, arrays, SQL, logic gates — I don't want
those to sound like twenty unrelated topics. [[beat:connect]] I want you to
see how they connect. [[beat:one_story]] Because there is a secret about
computer science that makes the subject much easier. It's not really a
collection of chapters. It's one story. And once you see that story, a
subject that looks complicated begins to become surprisingly logical.
[[beat:three_rules_tease]] But stay with me until the end, because I'm also
going to give you the three rules I want every one of my computer science
students to follow. If you follow those three rules throughout this course,
you'll never have to rely on blindly memorising page after page of notes.

[[beat:not_about_computers]] So let me begin with a statement that might
sound completely wrong. Computer science is not really about computers. A
computer is simply the machine we happen to use.
[[beat:about_information]] Computer science is about something much more
powerful. It's about information. How do we represent information? How do we
store it? How do we move it? How do we protect it? How do we make decisions
using it? [[beat:break_problem]] And perhaps most importantly, how do we
take a complicated problem and break it into steps that a machine can
follow? That is computer science. [[beat:before_watching]] Think about what
happened before you even started watching this lesson. You opened a website.
Perhaps you tapped a link. Perhaps you searched for the course. It looks
simple. But underneath that simple action, an extraordinary amount of
computer science took place. [[beat:underneath]] Your device represented
information using binary. Your network divided information into packets.
Those packets travelled across a network. Routers helped them find their
destination. Security systems helped protect the communication. Processors
executed instructions. Software controlled the hardware. And eventually
pixels on your screen and sound from your speakers created this lesson.
[[beat:pressed_play]] You didn't see any of that. You simply pressed play.
That is one of the fascinating things about computer science. The easier
technology becomes for the user, the more complicated the systems underneath
it often become. And during this course, we are going underneath.
[[beat:dont_memorise]] Don't memorise the machine. Understand the system.
That is the first principle of this course. I don't want you simply
memorising definitions so that you can reproduce them in an examination. I
want you to reach the point where the definitions make sense because you
understand the system.

[[beat:school_problem]] Imagine I give you the following problem. A school
has two thousand students. Every student sits several examinations. The
school wants to calculate grades, identify students who need support,
produce reports for parents, keep the information secure, and allow teachers
to access the correct records. How would you solve that problem? You could
employ hundreds of people, or you could design a system.
[[beat:data]] First, you have to determine what information you need.
Student name, student ID, subject, marks, grade. That is data.
[[beat:algorithm]] Then you have to decide how to store that data. You might
use a database. Then you need rules. If the mark is above a certain value,
assign one grade. If it is below another value, assign another. Those rules
form an algorithm. [[beat:program]] Then those instructions must be
converted into something a computer can execute. That becomes a program.
[[beat:system]] And when thousands of people are accessing that system, you
need hardware, networks, security, storage, software, backup,
authentication. Suddenly we have moved from one simple question — how do I
calculate a student's grade — to almost the entire field of computer
science. [[beat:daps]] Data, algorithm, program, system. That sequence is
worth remembering. A computer scientist learns to think across all four.

[[beat:two_worlds]] For this Cambridge O Level course, I want you to imagine
that computer science has two worlds. World one: how computers work. World
two: how we make computers solve problems. World one is computer systems.
World two is algorithms, programming and logic. And Cambridge tests those
two worlds separately. [[beat:paper1]] So Paper 1 is computer systems. Paper
1 lasts one hour and forty-five minutes. It contains seventy-five marks and
it counts for fifty percent of your qualification. It examines topics one
through six. All questions are compulsory. And there's another important
detail: you cannot use a calculator. [[beat:paper2]] Now Paper 2, algorithms
programming and logic, is also one hour and forty-five minutes,
seventy-five marks, and fifty percent of your qualification. It covers
topics seven through ten. Again, all questions are compulsory. Again, no
calculator. [[beat:scenario]] But Paper 2 has something important that Paper
1 does not. A major scenario-based programming problem appears at the end.
The final scenario question is worth fifteen marks, and Cambridge expects
students to spend approximately thirty minutes on it. The emphasis is on the
logic of the solution, rather than memorising the syntax of one particular
programming language. [[beat:fifty_fifty]] So already we know something
strategically important. Neither paper is the small paper. Neither paper is
the less important paper. They are equal. Fifty-fifty. Ignore one half of
this subject and you are giving away half of the qualification.

[[beat:the_map]] Now let's look at what we actually have to learn. Please
don't try to memorise this list right now. I want you to see the shape of
the journey. [[beat:paper1_topics]] Paper 1, computer systems, has data
representation, data transmission, hardware, software, the internet and its
uses, and automated and emerging technologies. Let's make those six areas
feel less intimidating. [[beat:topic1]] So topic one is data representation.
Here's the question: how does a computer represent the world? A computer
doesn't see the letter A in the way you do. You see a photograph, the
computer doesn't see a photograph. You hear music, the computer doesn't hear
music. Eventually everything must become data the machine can represent and
process. Numbers, text, images, sound, ultimately binary. Zeros and ones.
That is where our journey begins. [[beat:topic2]] Now suppose that data
needs to travel. You send somebody a photograph. Does the entire photograph
magically fly through the air? No. The data has to be transmitted. It may be
divided into packets. Those packets need addressing information. Errors can
occur. Data can become corrupted. Messages can fail to arrive. So the
computer needs mechanisms for detecting errors and recovering from them. And
if somebody intercepts our information, now we need encryption.
[[beat:topic3]] Now we meet the machine itself. The CPU, memory, storage,
input devices, output devices, sensors, network hardware. We will go inside
the processor and follow an instruction through the fetch, decode, execute
cycle. You will meet registers. You will understand why cache exists. You
will understand the difference between RAM and storage.
[[beat:topic4]] Hardware without software is an extremely expensive
collection of electronics. Software tells hardware what to do. So we will
examine operating systems, utility software, programming languages,
compilers, interpreters and interrupts. You'll see how software creates a
bridge between the human being and the machine. [[beat:topic5]] Topic five,
the internet and its uses. You probably use the internet every day, you're
using it right now. But could you explain what happens after you enter the
address of a website? What is the difference between the internet and the
World Wide Web? What does DNS do? What is a URL? Why do websites use
cookies? How do attackers steal information? What is phishing? What is
pharming? What is malware? And how do we defend ourselves? After this topic,
ordinary things you have used for years will suddenly make much more sense.
[[beat:topic6]] And topic six is automated and emerging technologies. Now
things become interesting. Sensors, microprocessors, actuators, automated
systems, robotics, artificial intelligence. We will investigate machines
that don't simply store information, but interact with the world around
them. [[beat:sense_process_act]] A temperature sensor detects heat. A
processor makes a decision. An actuator turns something on. The environment
changes. The sensor measures again. Sense, process, act, repeat. That is an
automated system. And that completes the first world: computer systems.

[[beat:world_two]] Now we enter the second world: algorithms, programming
and logic. And this world contains four topics. Algorithm design and
problem-solving. Programming. Databases. And Boolean logic.
[[beat:not_programming_first]] Notice something. We do not begin with
programming. We begin with problem-solving. That distinction matters. Bad
programmers sometimes start typing immediately. Good programmers ask: what
exactly is the problem? What information goes in? What result needs to come
out? What processing is required? Can I break this problem into smaller
pieces? Only then should we think about code. [[beat:problem_plan]] Problem,
plan, algorithm, code, test. That sequence is going to follow us throughout
Paper 2. [[beat:topic7]] An algorithm is essentially a precise sequence of
instructions for solving a problem. Imagine telling somebody how to make
tea. Make tea. That is not a useful algorithm. What kind of tea? How much
water? When do I boil it? When do I add the tea? How long do I wait?
[[beat:literal]] A computer has an even bigger problem. A human being can
guess what you meant. A computer cannot. Computers are extraordinarily
powerful, and extraordinarily literal. So our instructions must be precise.
[[beat:topic7_tools]] We will use pseudocode, flowcharts, trace tables,
validation, verification, test data, searching, sorting and decomposition.
Cambridge expects students not simply to recognise algorithms, but to write
and amend them for given problems and scenarios.
[[beat:topic8]] Topic eight is programming. This is where we convert
thinking into something executable. Variables, constants, data types, input,
output, selection, loops, strings, procedures, functions, arrays, files.
These words might sound intimidating now, but by the time we finish this
course, I want them to sound ordinary. [[beat:loop_selection]] Think about a
loop. If I tell you, write this sentence ten times, you already understand
repetition. Programming gives that idea a formal structure. If I say, if
your mark is at least eighty, display A — you already understand selection.
Programming isn't magic. Much of programming is taking logical ideas that
humans already understand and expressing them precisely enough for a
computer to execute. [[beat:topic9]] Modern life generates enormous amounts
of information. Schools, hospitals, banks, airlines, businesses,
governments, online stores — they all need organised data. So we learn about
fields, records, data types, primary keys and SQL. Instead of searching
through thousands of pieces of information manually — it's like looking for
a needle in a haystack — that's where databases come into play.
[[beat:topic10]] And finally, topic ten is Boolean logic. We arrive at
something that takes us almost back to where we began. True, false, one,
zero. Logic gates. AND, OR, NOT, NAND, NOR, XOR. Truth tables.
[[beat:simple_combined]] Computers appear fantastically complicated, but
underneath enormous systems are millions and billions of very simple logical
decisions. True or false. One or zero. Computer science repeatedly takes
something simple and combines it until something extraordinary emerges.

[[beat:three_skills]] Now I want you to think about the three skills
Cambridge actually tests. Knowing the syllabus is not enough. You need to
understand what Cambridge wants you to do with that knowledge. Cambridge
divides assessment into three objectives, but I'm going to translate them
into three ordinary words: know, apply, judge. [[beat:know]] Number one is
know. Cambridge calls it AO1. Can you demonstrate knowledge and
understanding? For example: what is RAM? What is phishing? What is a MAC
address? What is a compiler? Knowledge matters. But knowledge alone does not
produce the highest marks. [[beat:apply]] Number two, the skill you need is
apply. Cambridge calls this AO2. Can you use what you know in a situation
you haven't memorised? Maybe Cambridge describes a security system in a
warehouse. You have never seen that exact system before. But you understand
sensors. You understand processors. You understand actuators. Can you apply
those ideas? This becomes especially important in Paper 2.
[[beat:weightings]] Paper 1 places more weight on AO1, Paper 2 places more
weight on AO2. Across the qualification, AO1 and AO2 are each approximately
forty percent, with AO3 approximately twenty percent. So in simple language,
Paper 1 often asks: what do you know? Paper 2 asks: what can you do with
what you know? [[beat:judge]] Number three is judge. Cambridge calls it AO3.
Evaluate. Make a reasoned judgement. Reach a conclusion. Suppose a company
is considering replacing workers with an automated system. A weak answer
would say: automation is good because it is faster. That's not enough.
[[beat:evaluation_chain]] A stronger answer asks: faster in what way? What
happens to accuracy? What happens to cost? What happens to jobs? What
happens if the system fails? What happens to security? Who is affected? And
finally, given this particular situation, what conclusion follows? Claim,
reason, context, conclusion. That is evaluation.

[[beat:fool]] Now let me give you a warning. Computer science can fool
students. They read a chapter, everything makes sense. They look at their
notes, everything still makes sense. They watch a video, they think: yes, I
understand that. Then they open a past paper, and suddenly they cannot
answer the question. [[beat:recognise_retrieve]] Why? Because recognising an
idea is not the same as being able to retrieve and apply it. That is one of
the biggest traps in this subject. So there are four habits we are going to
avoid, and I'm calling them traps. [[beat:trap1]] Trap one: memorising
without understanding. If you memorise that RAM is volatile memory, but you
don't understand what volatile means, the moment Cambridge changes the
wording of the question, you are in trouble. [[beat:trap2]] Trap two:
understanding without precision. Sometimes you know roughly what something
means, but examination marks are awarded for particular ideas. The router
sends internet around — you might understand roughly what a router does, but
that sentence is too vague. We need technical language. We need precision.
[[beat:trap3]] Trap three: watching without practising. You cannot learn
programming by watching someone else program, just as you cannot learn to
swim by watching swimming videos. At some point, you have to get into the
water. Cambridge explicitly describes computer science as a practical
subject, and expects students to have experience writing, running, testing
and debugging programs. So throughout this course, we will actually solve
problems. [[beat:trap4]] What is trap four? Waiting until the end to use
past papers. Many students message me: sir, please give me a past paper.
Past paper skills should not begin after the syllabus finishes. They should
begin while we are learning. You learn the concept, then you learn how
Cambridge asks about the concept, then you learn how marks are awarded.
[[beat:learn_use]] Learn it, use it, explain it, examine it. That's a simple
formula.

[[beat:teaching_method]] So how will we learn? Here's how I'm going to teach
you, and it has been successful for most of my students. I'm not going to
begin every topic by throwing definitions onto the screen. We are going to
begin with a problem. A situation. A story. Something familiar. Then we
build the idea. Then we give that idea its technical name. Then we connect
it to another idea. Then we apply it. And finally, we turn it into
examination language. [[beat:five_steps]] Understand. Visualise. Connect.
Apply. Answer like an exam candidate. [[beat:packets_example]] Let me give
you an example. Later, when we learn about packets, I'm not simply going to
tell you that a packet is a unit of data transmitted across a network.
Instead, imagine I give you a five-hundred-page book, and I tell you that
the postal service will allow only ten pages in each envelope. Suddenly we
have problems. We need many envelopes. We need to know where they are going.
We need to know which pages belong in which order. One envelope might
disappear. Different envelopes might take different routes. At the
destination, somebody has to reconstruct the book. Now you already
understand why packets need certain information. The technical terminology
comes after the mental model.

[[beat:programming_promise]] And now I want to speak specifically to
students who are worried about programming. Perhaps you've never programmed
before. Perhaps pseudocode looks like a foreign language. Or perhaps you
think: I'm just not a programmer. I don't want you to think that way,
because programming is a skill, and skills improve through deliberate
practice. [[beat:start_small]] So we will start small. Store one value.
Display one value. Make one decision. Repeat one instruction. Then combine
them. [[beat:build_up]] One decision becomes an IF statement. Repetition
becomes a loop. Repeated logic becomes a procedure. Many related values
become an array. Information that needs to survive after the program ends
goes into a file. And eventually the small pieces combine into a complete
solution. [[beat:logic_first]] Cambridge makes an important point about
Paper 2. Practical programming experience matters, but logic is more
important than programming language syntax. Logic first. Syntax second. We
learn how programmers think, then we learn how to express that thinking
precisely.

[[beat:roadmap_q]] So now you're asking, what is your roadmap? At the lowest
level — before databases, before artificial intelligence, before
cybersecurity, before programming — we begin with a question. What language
does a computer actually understand? [[beat:binary]] Zero and one. Binary.
[[beat:roadmap]] From there we'll move outward. Binary becomes data. Data
becomes text, images and sound. Data travels across networks. Hardware
processes it. Software controls the hardware. Networks connect machines.
Security protects information. Automated systems interact with the physical
world. Then we turn the entire process around. Instead of asking how does
the computer work, we ask: how do I make the computer solve my problem?
Algorithms. Programming. Databases. Logic. Bits, data, systems, networks,
algorithms, programs, solutions. That is our journey.

[[beat:rule1]] Before we finish this introduction, I want you to remember
three things. Rule one: ask why. When I tell you something, don't be
satisfied merely knowing that it happens. Ask why. Why binary? Why packets?
Why cache? Why encryption? Why validation? Why loops? The word why turns
facts into understanding. [[beat:rule2]] Rule two is connect everything.
Computer science is not ten disconnected chapters. The topics talk to each
other. Hexadecimal will appear again when we study things such as MAC
addresses. Sensors from hardware will return when we study automated
systems. Boolean logic will connect to programming. File handling will
connect to data. Cybersecurity will connect to networks. Every time you
connect two ideas, both ideas become easier to remember.
[[beat:rule3]] And rule number three is solve problems. Computer science is
not a spectator sport. You have to calculate, convert, trace, design, write,
debug, evaluate — and sometimes get something completely wrong.
[[beat:not_failure]] That's totally fine, because then you're going to find
out why. That is not failure. That is programming.

[[beat:closing]] At the beginning of this lesson I told you that computer
science is not really about computers. Now perhaps you understand what I
meant. It's about information, logic, systems and problem-solving. A
computer is simply one of the most powerful tools humanity has created for
putting those ideas into action. [[beat:think_like]] By the end of this
course, I don't want you merely recognising computer science terminology. I
want you to look at a system and ask: what information is going in? How is
it represented? How is it processed? How is it stored? How is it
transmitted? How is it protected? And most importantly, how could I design a
better solution? Think like a computer scientist. That is the goal.
[[beat:next]] And we start with perhaps the simplest idea in the entire
course. Two symbols. Zero and one. In our first lesson, we enter the world
of binary. [[beat:thanks]] Thank you for joining. Next week we'll dive into
chapter one. I look forward to seeing you all there.
