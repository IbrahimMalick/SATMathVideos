[[beat:title]] Welcome back, everyone. Today, we are going to master one of
the most important skills on the Digital SAT Reading and Writing section.
Transitions. [[beat:sounds_right]] Now, many students believe transition
questions are easy. They look at the answer choices and say: That sounds
right. But that is exactly what the SAT wants you to do. It wants you to
choose an answer because it sounds good. Today, we are going to stop
guessing. We are going to use logic. [[beat:formula]] Here is the formula
for today's lesson: Relationship first. Transition second. Punctuation
third. Remember those three steps. Relationship. Transition. Punctuation.

[[beat:airport]] Let us begin with a story. Imagine that you have just
landed at a very large international airport. You leave the airplane and
enter the terminal. Normally, you would see signs telling you where to go.
Immigration. Baggage claim. Connecting flights. Restaurants. And the exit.
[[beat:lost]] Now imagine that every sign has disappeared. You walk down one
hallway. You turn left. You climb two escalators. And somehow, twenty
minutes later, you are standing beside the same airplane again.
[[beat:signs]] That is what a paragraph feels like without transitions.
Transitions are directional signs for the reader. They tell us where the
writer's thinking is going. A transition may tell us: I am adding another
idea. Here is an example. This happened because of the previous idea. Or: Be
careful. The direction is about to change. [[beat:job]] Your job on the SAT
is not to choose the fanciest word. Your job is to identify the direction of
the writer's thinking.

[[beat:strategy]] Here is the most important strategy in this lesson. Do not
begin with the answer choices. Let me say that again. Do not begin with the
answer choices. Why? Because once you see the choices, your brain may try to
make every answer sound possible. [[beat:example1]] Let us look at an
example. I studied for six hours. Blank. I failed the exam.
[[beat:expectation]] Before looking at any choices, what relationship do you
notice? The first sentence creates an expectation. If someone studies for
six hours, we expect that person to perform well. But the second sentence
says: I failed the exam. That result goes against our expectation. So we
need a transition that shows contrast. [[beat:example1_answer]] A good
answer would be: I studied for six hours. However, I failed the exam.
[[beat:choices1]] Now suppose the choices are: A. Therefore. B. Furthermore.
C. However. D. For example. The correct answer is C. However.
[[beat:invent]] But when students stare at the choices for too long, they
sometimes begin inventing strange explanations. They might say: Maybe the
student studied for so long that he became tired. Maybe the tiredness caused
him to fail. So perhaps therefore could work. No. Do not invent a new story
to rescue a wrong answer. Use the information that is already in the
passage.

[[beat:method]] Here is the method you should use on every transition
question. [[beat:m1]] Step one. Read the full passage. Do not read only the
sentence that contains the blank. The relationship may depend on information
from the previous sentence. [[beat:m2]] Step two. Ignore the blank. Read the
two ideas separately. [[beat:m3]] Step three. Describe the relationship
using simple language. Ask yourself: Is the writer adding information? Is
the writer giving an example? Is the writer showing a result? Is the writer
changing direction? Is the writer clarifying an idea? Or is the writer
describing a sequence? You can use very simple phrases in your mind. Same
direction. Opposite direction. This caused that. Here comes an example.
[[beat:m4]] Step four. Now look at the answer choices. Choose the transition
that performs the job you already identified. You are no longer asking:
Which word sounds good? You are asking: Which word does the job I need? That
is a much easier question.

[[beat:families]] Most SAT transition questions belong to three major
families. Think of these as three families living in the same neighborhood.
The first family agrees with everyone. The second family causes everything.
And the third family argues with everyone. Let us meet the first family.

[[beat:cont]] Continuers move in the same general direction as the previous
idea. They may: Add information. Give an example. Clarify an idea. Emphasize
something. Or show a sequence. [[beat:add_words]] Transitions that add
information include: Moreover. Furthermore. In addition. Also. And
additionally. [[beat:malik]] Here is an example. Malik is an excellent
mathematics student. Moreover, he is an accomplished musician. The second
sentence adds another positive fact about Malik. It does not disagree with
the first sentence. It is not caused by the first sentence. It simply adds
more information. [[beat:restaurant]] Here is another example. Our
restaurant serves delicious food. In addition, we have free parking. The
free parking was not caused by the delicious food. It does not contradict
the delicious food. It is simply another reason to visit the restaurant. And
depending on the city, free parking may be the most impressive part.
[[beat:example_words]] Transitions that introduce examples include: For
example. For instance. Specifically. And to illustrate. Listen to this
sentence. Some animals use tools to obtain food. For example, certain
chimpanzees use sticks to collect termites. The first sentence gives us a
broad idea. Some animals use tools. The second sentence gives us one
specific example. Chimpanzees are one example of animals that use tools.
Here is a useful question to ask: Is the second idea one specific example of
the larger idea? If the answer is yes, you probably need an example
transition. [[beat:clarify]] Some transitions clarify or strengthen an idea.
These include: In fact. Indeed. In other words. And that is. Here is an
example. The scientist's discovery was significant. In fact, it changed how
researchers understood the disease. The second sentence strengthens the
first statement. [[beat:clarify2]] Now listen to this example. The device is
portable. In other words, users can easily carry it from one location to
another. The second sentence restates the first idea more clearly. Be
careful. In fact and in other words are not always interchangeable. In fact
usually emphasizes or strengthens an idea. In other words usually restates
or explains an idea. They belong to the same general family. But they do not
perform exactly the same job. [[beat:sequence]] Some transitions show time
or order. These include: First. Next. Subsequently. Previously. Finally. And
meanwhile. For example: The researchers collected soil samples. Next, they
analyzed the samples in a laboratory. The ideas are connected by order.
Think about a cooking video. First, add the flour. Next, add the eggs.
Finally, call your mother because you have forgotten how to cook. That is
sequence. It may not be a successful recipe. But the transitions are
excellent.

[[beat:cause]] Now let us meet the second transition family. Cause and
effect transitions. These transitions show that the second idea is a result
of the first. Common cause and effect transitions include: Therefore.
Consequently. Thus. Hence. Accordingly. And as a result. [[beat:ice]] Here
is an example. The road was covered with ice. Therefore, the school closed
for the day. The icy road caused, or strongly contributed to, the school
closing. [[beat:sara]] Here is another example. Sara forgot to charge her
phone. Consequently, she could not use it during the trip. The dead phone is
a result of Sara not charging it. [[beat:domino]] You can use what I call
the domino test. Imagine two dominoes. The first event falls. Then it knocks
down the second event. Ask yourself: Did the first idea produce the second
idea? If the answer is yes, look for a cause and effect transition.
[[beat:price_sales]] Consider this example. The company reduced the price of
its product. Blank. Sales increased. The price reduction may have caused the
increase in sales. A logical answer is: As a result, sales increased.
[[beat:price_logo]] Now consider this example. The company reduced the price
of its product. Blank. It changed the color of its logo. Did lowering the
price cause the company to change its logo? Probably not. The second
sentence may add another fact. But it is not necessarily a result.
[[beat:sequence_rule]] Remember this important rule: Just because one event
happens after another does not mean that the first event caused the second.
Sequence is not automatically cause and effect.

[[beat:contra]] Now let us meet the third family. The contradictors.
Contradictors signal that the writer is changing direction. Common contrast
transitions include: However. Nevertheless. Nonetheless. In contrast. On the
other hand. Still. And yet. [[beat:apartment]] Here is an example. The
apartment is small. However, it receives plenty of natural light. The second
sentence changes direction. A small apartment may sound negative. But plenty
of natural light sounds positive. The ideas are not complete opposites. But
they create a contrast. [[beat:surprise]] You can use what I call the
surprise test. Ask yourself: Does the second idea challenge what I expected
after reading the first? [[beat:runner]] Here is an example. The runner
injured her ankle shortly before the race. Blank. She finished in first
place. What would you expect after reading the first sentence? You might
expect the runner to lose. You might expect her to struggle. You might even
expect her to withdraw from the race. Instead, she finished in first place.
That is a surprise. A strong transition would be: Nevertheless, she finished
in first place. [[beat:however_vs]] Now let us compare however and in
contrast. These words are similar. But they are not always identical.
However can introduce a general change in direction. For example: The
software is expensive. However, it is extremely reliable. In contrast often
compares two different people, objects, groups, or places. For example:
Desert temperatures can exceed forty five degrees Celsius. In contrast,
polar regions remain extremely cold. When two separate subjects are being
directly compared, in contrast may be more precise.

[[beat:detective]] Now it is time to become a transition detective. Imagine
that you are Detective Transition. Your mission is to examine two statements
and identify the relationship between them. [[beat:case1]] Case number one.
Some students believe that reading quickly is the best way to finish the SAT
on time. Blank. Reading too quickly can cause students to miss important
details. What do we have? The first statement presents a belief. The second
statement presents a problem with that belief. The direction changes. We
need a contrast transition. A logical answer is: However, reading too
quickly can cause students to miss important details. [[beat:case2]] Case
number two. The museum wanted to attract more young visitors. Blank. It
created an interactive mobile app. Why did the museum create the app?
Because it wanted to attract younger visitors. The second action is a result
of the first idea. A logical answer is: Therefore, it created an interactive
mobile app. [[beat:case3]] Case number three. Many birds migrate over long
distances. Blank. The Arctic tern travels from the Arctic to the Antarctic
and back. The Arctic tern is a specific example of a bird that migrates a
long distance. A logical answer is: For example, the Arctic tern travels
from the Arctic to the Antarctic and back. Notice what we did. We identified
the job first. Then we selected the transition. That is the method.

[[beat:punct]] Now we need to discuss punctuation. This is where good
students sometimes lose points. You may understand the relationship
perfectly. You may select the correct transition word. And you may still
choose the wrong answer because of punctuation. [[beat:conj]] Words such as:
However. Therefore. Moreover. Consequently. And nevertheless. Are often
called conjunctive adverbs. These words cannot join two complete sentences
using only a comma. [[beat:splice]] Listen to this incorrect sentence. The
city is old, however, it contains many modern buildings. This sentence is
incorrect. Why? Because: The city is old is a complete sentence. And: It
contains many modern buildings is also a complete sentence. A single comma
is not strong enough to join them. The comma is trying its best. But it
simply does not have the qualifications for the job. This error is called a
comma splice. [[beat:pattern1]] Here is one correct pattern. The city is
old. However, it contains many modern buildings. A period separates the two
complete sentences. However begins the second sentence and is followed by a
comma. [[beat:pattern2]] Here is another correct pattern. The city is old;
however, it contains many modern buildings. A semicolon connects the two
complete sentences. Remember this pattern: Complete sentence. Semicolon.
Transition. Comma. Complete sentence. Or remember it this way: Semicolon
before. Comma after. [[beat:interrupt]] A transition can also appear as an
interruption in the middle of a sentence. For example: The city is old; it
does, however, contain many modern buildings. The word however interrupts
the sentence. Because it is nonessential, it is surrounded by commas. You
can remove it, and the sentence still works. The city is old; it does
contain many modern buildings. [[beat:fanboys]] Now let us discuss FANBOYS.
FANBOYS stands for: For. And. Nor. But. Or. Yet. So. These are coordinating
conjunctions. They can join two complete sentences when they are preceded by
a comma. For example: The city is old, but it contains many modern
buildings. That sentence is correct. [[beat:fanboys2]] Now compare these two
patterns. The city is old, but it contains modern buildings. And: The city
is old; however, it contains modern buildings. Do not treat however like
but. This sentence is incorrect: The city is old, however it contains modern
buildings. This sentence is correct: The city is old, but it contains modern
buildings. And this sentence is also correct: The city is old; however, it
contains modern buildings.

[[beat:shortcut]] Now let us discuss one of the fastest elimination
strategies. The synonym shortcut. Suppose the choices are: A. Therefore. B.
Consequently. C. However. D. For example. Therefore and consequently usually
perform the same basic job. Both show a result. Can both be correct? No. The
SAT gives you only one correct answer. So when two answer choices are true
functional synonyms, and they fit the sentence in exactly the same way, they
usually eliminate each other. [[beat:pairs]] This is especially useful with
pairs such as: Therefore and consequently. However and nevertheless.
Moreover and furthermore. For example and for instance. [[beat:caution]] But
use this shortcut carefully. Some transitions may look similar without doing
exactly the same job. For example: In fact emphasizes. In other words
restates. For example provides an illustration. All three may continue the
discussion. But they perform different functions. The safest method is:
First, determine the relationship. Second, notice any true synonym pairs.
Third, eliminate choices that cannot be uniquely correct.

[[beat:antarctica]] Now let us work through an Antarctica example. Imagine
that a passage explains the climate of Antarctica. Conditions in the
interior of Antarctica are extremely inhospitable. Temperatures remain far
below freezing. The air is exceptionally dry. And strong winds are common.
Blank. The Antarctic Peninsula experiences relatively mild temperatures and
contains areas of liquid water. [[beat:ant_ignore]] First, ignore the blank.
Interior Antarctica is extremely inhospitable. The Antarctic Peninsula is
relatively mild. Are these ideas moving in the same direction? No. Does one
idea cause the other? No. Is the peninsula an example of the harsh interior?
Definitely not. The two regions are being compared. Their conditions are
different. We need a contrast transition. [[beat:ant_choices]] Suppose the
choices are: A. Therefore. B. For instance. C. Indeed. D. In contrast.
[[beat:ant_elimA]] Let us test them. Therefore would mean that the harsh
interior conditions caused the peninsula to have mild temperatures. That
does not make sense. Eliminate it. [[beat:ant_elimB]] For instance would
mean that the mild peninsula is an example of the inhospitable interior. It
is not. Eliminate it. [[beat:ant_elimC]] Indeed would strengthen or confirm
the first idea. But the second sentence presents a different situation.
Eliminate it. [[beat:ant_answer]] In contrast signals a direct comparison
between the interior and the peninsula. That is the correct answer. Choice
D. In contrast.
