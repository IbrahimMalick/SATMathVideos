[[beat:title]] Welcome to our first major module in the SAT Math preparation
series: Algebra. [[beat:scope]] Algebra is one of the most important areas of
mathematics you will study in school, and it represents a major part of the
Digital SAT Math section. For the SAT, algebra primarily involves linear
expressions, linear equations, linear inequalities, systems of equations, and
linear functions. [[beat:why]] But algebra is not simply a collection of rules
for moving numbers and letters around. It is a way of describing
relationships, identifying patterns, and solving real-world problems. In this
lecture, we will examine the core ideas that will help you approach SAT
algebra questions with greater confidence, accuracy, and speed.

[[beat:two_ways]] Algebra can be understood through two closely connected
perspectives: equations and functions. [[beat:eq_example]] An equation usually
asks you to find an unknown value. For example: three x plus five equals
twenty. Here, your goal is to determine the value of x that makes the
equation true. [[beat:fn_example]] A function, however, describes how one
quantity changes in relation to another. For example: y equals three x plus
five. Instead of finding only one value of x, this equation describes an
entire relationship between x and y. [[beat:both]] On the SAT, you must be
comfortable with both perspectives. Some questions will ask you to solve for
a particular variable. Others will ask you to interpret what a variable,
coefficient, slope, or intercept means in a real-world situation.

[[beat:equivalence]] The most important principle in solving equations is
equivalence. An equation tells us that two expressions have the same value.
You can think of it as a perfectly balanced scale. Whatever you do to one
side of the equation, you must also do to the other side.
[[beat:solve_setup]] Consider: x plus seven equals fifteen.
[[beat:solve_sub]] To isolate x, subtract seven from both sides.
[[beat:solve_result]] Therefore, x equals eight. We are not magically moving
the seven to the other side. We are performing the same operation on both
sides to preserve the equality. This way of thinking is much safer than
memorising phrases such as move it across and change the sign. Those
shortcuts may work in simple problems, but they often lead to mistakes in
more complicated equations. [[beat:check]] After solving an equation, develop
the habit of checking your answer in the original equation. Substitute x
equals eight: eight plus seven is fifteen. Both sides are equal, so the
solution is correct. Checking your answer can take only a few seconds and may
protect you from losing an easy SAT point.

[[beat:three_reps]] A function can usually be represented in three main ways:
symbolically, with an equation. Tabularly, with a table. And graphically,
with a graph. Strong SAT students can move comfortably between all three
representations. [[beat:gym_eq]] Imagine that a gym charges a ten dollar
joining fee and then fifteen dollars per month. We can represent the total
cost with the equation: c equals ten plus fifteen m. Here, c represents the
total cost, m represents the number of months, ten represents the initial
joining fee, and fifteen represents the monthly charge. [[beat:gym_table]]
Now let us represent the same relationship in a table. Zero months costs ten
dollars. One month costs twenty five dollars. Two months, forty dollars.
Three months, fifty five dollars. Notice that the total cost increases by
fifteen dollars whenever the number of months increases by one.
[[beat:gym_graph]] On a graph, this relationship appears as a straight line.
The graph begins at the point zero comma ten. The value ten is the
y-intercept, because it represents the cost when the number of months is
zero. [[beat:gym_slope]] The line then rises by fifteen units for every one
unit increase in the number of months. This rate of change is the slope.
[[beat:same_15_eq]] The same number, fifteen, appears in three different
forms. In the equation, it is the coefficient of m. [[beat:same_15_table]] In
the table, it is the repeated increase in cost. [[beat:same_15_graph]] On the
graph, it is the slope of the line. These are not separate ideas. They are
three different ways of expressing the same mathematical relationship.
[[beat:connect]] The Digital SAT frequently asks you to connect these
representations. You may need to select the graph that matches an equation,
construct an equation from a table, or interpret the slope and intercept in a
word problem.

[[beat:linear]] A linear function describes a relationship with a constant
rate of change. Suppose a delivery company charges a fixed service fee of
twenty dollars plus four dollars for every mile travelled. The total cost can
be written as: c equals twenty plus four m. The total cost increases by
exactly four dollars for every additional mile. That constant increase is
what makes the relationship linear. [[beat:diff_table]] In a table, a linear
relationship has a constant first difference. If the input values increase by
equal amounts, the output values also change by equal amounts. For example:
when x is one, y is seven. When x is two, y is ten. When x is three, y is
thirteen. And when x is four, y is sixteen. [[beat:diff_mark]] Each time x
increases by one, y increases by three. Therefore, the rate of change is
constant, and the relationship is linear. [[beat:nonlinear]] Later, you will
study nonlinear relationships such as quadratic and exponential functions. In
those functions, the outputs do not increase by the same amount each time.
For now, remember this key idea: [[beat:key_idea]] a straight-line
relationship has a constant rate of change.

[[beat:word]] Many students understand equations but struggle when those
equations are hidden inside a paragraph. The key is to identify two pieces of
information: the starting value, and the rate of change. [[beat:stream]]
Consider this situation. A streaming service charges a one-time activation
fee of twelve dollars and a monthly subscription fee of eight dollars. The
starting value is twelve, and the monthly rate is eight. [[beat:stream_eq]]
The equation is: c equals twelve plus eight m. [[beat:mxb]] In a linear
equation written as y equals m x plus b, the value m represents the slope or
rate of change, while b represents the initial value or y-intercept.
[[beat:m_warning]] Be careful: the letter m in the formula y equals m x plus
b traditionally represents slope. In a word problem, however, a variable such
as m may also stand for months. Always use the meaning given in the question.

[[beat:strategy]] There is rarely only one way to solve an SAT algebra
problem. You may be able to: [[beat:strat_list]] solve the equation
symbolically. Sketch a graph. Create a small table. Substitute the answer
choices. Test convenient values. Or use the built-in Desmos graphing
calculator. [[beat:efficient]] Your goal is not to use the most complicated
method. Your goal is to use the method that is fast, accurate, and
appropriate for the problem. For a simple equation, algebraic manipulation
may be quickest. For a system of equations, graphing may reveal the
intersection immediately. For a question with numerical answer choices,
substituting the choices may save time. For a function question, a table may
make the pattern easier to recognise. Strong test-takers do not rely on only
one technique. They examine the structure of the problem and select the most
efficient approach.

[[beat:review]] Before we finish, remember the central ideas from this
lecture. [[beat:rev1]] First, equations and functions give us two different
ways to study algebra. Equations help us find unknown values, while functions
describe relationships between changing quantities. [[beat:rev2]] Second,
solving equations depends on equivalence. Whatever operation you perform must
preserve the equality of both sides. [[beat:rev3]] Third, functions can be
represented with equations, tables, and graphs. You should be able to move
between all three. [[beat:rev4]] Fourth, linear relationships have a constant
rate of change. That constant appears as the coefficient in an equation, the
first difference in a table, and the slope of a graph. [[beat:rev5]] Finally,
remain flexible. The SAT rewards students who can choose an efficient
strategy rather than mechanically applying the same method to every question.
[[beat:next]] In the next lessons, we will apply these principles to solving
linear equations, working with inequalities, interpreting slope and
intercepts, and solving systems of equations. [[beat:pillars]] Keep the three
pillars of SAT algebra in mind: equivalence, multiple representations, and
constant change. Once these ideas become familiar, algebra stops looking like
a collection of confusing symbols and starts becoming a logical system that
you can control.
