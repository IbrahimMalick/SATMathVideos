[[beat:represent_intro]] Let's begin with represent. Suppose I write a half,

[[beat:three_outfits]] Three different numbers? No. One number wearing three

Fractions, decimals and percentages are different representations of the

[[beat:benchmarks]] So there are some mental benchmarks. You should have

[[beat:multiplier_intro]] All right, now we are going to make percentages
considerably more powerful. Suppose a jacket costs eighty dollars, and
there's a twenty percent discount. What is the new price? Now, most students
would do twenty percent of eighty equals sixteen, then eighty minus sixteen

arrived at the right number. [[beat:multiplier]] But I want to show you a
more powerful way of thinking. If twenty percent disappeared, what
percentage remains? That's eighty percent. And eighty percent as a decimal

times zero point eight, we get sixty four. So that zero point eight is
called a multiplier. [[beat:increase]] Now suppose something costs eighty

That would only calculate the increase. The new value is a hundred percent

two. Therefore eighty times one point two equals ninety six.
[[beat:multiplier_rule]] Remember: increase by twenty percent, multiply by
one point two. Decrease by twenty percent, multiply by zero point eight.

[[beat:pct_trap]] Now I have a question that catches many students. A phone

though we should be. Plus twenty percent, minus twenty percent, they should
cancel out. Except they don't. [[beat:pct_trap_calc]] Let's calculate. A
hundred increased by twenty percent is a hundred times one point two, so the
new value is a hundred and twenty. Now decrease a hundred and twenty by
twenty percent, so a hundred and twenty times zero point eight is ninety
six. We are at ninety six, not at a hundred. [[beat:base_matters]] Why?
Because the two percentages were applied to different starting values.
Remember that sentence. So the key thing is, percentages depend on their
base. Twenty percent of a hundred is twenty. Twenty percent of a hundred and
twenty is twenty four. [[beat:not_reversible]] So percentage increase and
decrease are not automatically reversible. That's what you need to
understand, and that's the mind frame you need to be in.

[[beat:neg_intro]] All right, now let's stop memorizing signs. Let's switch
to something that causes enormous confusion. Suppose five minus eight. Think
of temperature. You are at five degrees centigrade, the temperature drops
eight degrees, and you arrive at minus three degrees centigrade. So five
minus eight equals minus three. Nothing mysterious.
[[beat:neg_numberline]] Now let's look at minus four plus seven. You are at
minus four on the number line. You move seven places, so you reach three.
Again, very simple. [[beat:neg_pattern]] Now, students are usually taught
negative times negative equals positive. That's fine. But students memorize
it and then forget it. Instead, look for a pattern. Three times two equals
six. Two times two equals four. One times two equals two. Zero times two
equals zero. Minus one times two equals minus two. And minus two times two
equals minus four. The pattern forces the result.
[[beat:neg_pattern2]] Now consider three times minus two, minus six. Two
times minus two, minus four. One times minus two is minus two. And zero
times minus two is zero. What must come next? Minus one times minus two —
and that would be what? Plus two. [[beat:sign_rule]] So mathematics is all
about maintaining the pattern. Same signs, positive. Different signs,
negative. This works for multiplication and division.
[[beat:bracket_trap]] All right, here's a classic exam trap. Calculate minus
three to the power two. Is that nine? No. The square applies to three, not
automatically to the negative sign. So minus three squared means the
negative of three squared, and that equals minus nine. But if you have,
inside brackets, minus three, to the power two, that means you're
multiplying minus three times minus three, so that will be positive nine.
The brackets completely change the answer. Put these side by side. Minus
three squared equals minus nine. Inside the brackets, minus three squared
equals nine. One pair of brackets, completely different results.

[[beat:powers_intro]] Now let's look at powers. Two to the power three does
not mean two times three. It means two times two times two, so that's eight.
The small number is the index, or exponent. It tells us how many times the
base appears as a factor. [[beat:squares_roots]] So now let's look at
squares and square roots. Five to the power two equals twenty five.
Therefore the square root of twenty five is five. These are inverse
operations. Squaring takes us one way, square rooting brings us back. Think
of these as mathematical opposites. [[beat:cubes]] All right, cubes and cube
roots. Three cubed equals twenty seven. Therefore the cube root of twenty
seven is three. Again, one operation builds, the other one reverses.
[[beat:pm_root]] Now here's the crucial distinction. What is the square root
of forty nine? Seven. But if I ask, x to the power two equals forty nine,
then x equals seven or minus seven. Why? Because seven squared equals forty
nine, and minus seven, inside the bracket, squared, also equals forty nine.
This distinction becomes increasingly important in algebra.

[[beat:index_mult]] All right. Now consider this: two cubed times two to the
power four. You could calculate two cubed as eight, and two to the power
four is sixteen. Now eight times sixteen is what? A hundred and twenty
eight. But look underneath. Two to the power three is two times two times
two, and two to the power four is two times two times two times two.
Together we have seven twos. Therefore two to the power three times two to
the power four equals two to the power seven. This gives us what? When
multiplying powers with the same base, you add the indices. A to the power m
times a to the power n equals a to the power m plus n.
[[beat:index_div]] All right, now let's do the division. Two to the power
seven divided by two to the power three. Cancel the twos. What remains?
Four. Therefore two to the power seven divided by two to the power three
equals two to the power four. So when dividing powers with the same base,
subtract the indices. When we were multiplying with the same base, we were
adding the indices, and when we are dividing with the same base, we are
subtracting the indices. [[beat:zero_index]] So let's look at the zero
index. What is five to the power zero? Watch this. Five cubed divided by
five cubed is what? One. Using our index rule, five to the power three minus
three equals five to the power zero. Therefore five to the power zero equals
one. In fact, a to the power zero equals one, provided a is not equal to
zero. Now don't just memorize it. Now you know where it comes from.

[[beat:std_form_why]] All right, now we're going to do standard form.
Imagine writing the distance of a hundred and fifty million kilometres,
that's roughly the scale of the Earth to Sun distance. And now imagine
working with zero point zero zero zero zero zero zero one. Numbers like
these become awkward very quickly, and scientists needed a compact language.
That's where standard form comes in. [[beat:std_form_def]] A standard form
looks like a times ten to the power n, where one is less than or equal to a,
and a is less than ten. That last condition is important.
[[beat:std_big]] Take forty five thousand. Move the decimal point until the
first number is between one and ten. So that gives us four point five. We
moved four places. Therefore we can write forty five thousand as four point
five times ten to the power four. [[beat:std_small]] Now how do we do it
with zero point zero zero zero seven two? We're going to move the decimal
until we have seven point two. So we moved four places. Therefore zero point
zero zero zero seven two equals seven point two times ten to the power minus
four. [[beat:std_intuition]] Here's a useful intuition. Huge number,
positive power. Tiny number, negative power. [[beat:std_trap]] All right,
let me ask you this. Is forty five times ten cubed in standard form? It may
be mathematically correct, but it is not standard form, because forty five
is not between one and ten. If you rewrite it as four point five times ten
to the power four, now it is in standard form.

[[beat:rounding_intro]] All right, now we're going to do rounding and
estimation, and the art of being approximately right. Students sometimes
think an approximate answer is an inferior answer. Not at all. Sometimes
approximation is exactly what mathematics requires.
[[beat:decimal_places]] Suppose the calculator tells you seven point eight
four six two nine one, dot dot dot. Do you always need all those digits? No.
If I ask for two decimal places, look at seven point eight four. Then
inspect the next digit. The third decimal digit is six. Five or more, round
up. So seven point eight five. [[beat:sig_figs]] Now suppose I ask seven
thousand eight hundred and forty six to two significant figures. Significant
figures begin from the first non-zero digit. Seven is first. Eight is
second. Look at the next digit: four. So we keep the eight. The answer is
seven thousand eight hundred. [[beat:sf_distinction]] Do you understand?
Notice the distinction. Decimal places count from the decimal point.
Significant figures count from the first non-zero digit.
[[beat:sf_small]] All right, now let's look at small numbers. Take zero
point zero zero four seven six three. Two significant figures would be what?
Do the zeros count? Not those leading zeros. The first significant digit is
four. Second is seven. The next digit is six, so round the seven upward. The
answer would be zero point zero zero four eight.

[[beat:estimation]] So when we're doing estimation, we calculate
approximately. Here's an example. Nineteen point eight times four point nine

calculator, estimate. Nineteen point eight is approximately twenty. Four
point nine one is approximately five. And zero point two zero three is
approximately zero point two. So if I were to write twenty times five

mathematical lie detector. It asks: does this answer make sense? And that
question can save marks.
